# Linus Torvalds: frameworks

Built from 34 accepted distinct works and 159,314 source words. Compiled reasoning with source support and limits below.

- **inferred**: Unnamed — user-workflow regression triage. First identify the existing workflow that changed, including incidental behavior users came to rely on. For an ordinary feature or fix, treat disruption as a defect and look for a compatible implementation; if the release is near and the interaction is unresolved, revert the exposing change and revisit it after investigating alternatives. Act promptly because users may also begin relying on the new behavior. A serious security hazard can justify an exception, but seek a mitigation that preserves legitimate use. The 2019 revert concerned an I/O improvement; the supplied announcement does not establish the full underlying bug. - **inferred**: Unnamed — configuration migration test. When renaming or splitting configuration options, start with an existing working configuration and ask whether its essential behavior survives an update. Carry the old option forward as a default for the corresponding replacement; do not solve the problem by enabling every newly introduced, unrelated driver. Then test moves both backward and forward through history: a configuration can silently disappear during bisection even when a forward upgrade looks correct. Torvalds treated sound Kconfig reorganization as an avoidable inconvenience, while explicitly qualifying it as not quite a regression. - **inferred**: Unnamed — staged, time-based release. Develop and test changes in public trees before the merge window, accept ready work during an approximately two-week window, and spend the following release candidates mostly stabilizing. Judge late changes by their urgency and the chance that their fix creates another regression; a noncritical fix can wait for the next frequent release. Disable or revert an unresolved feature rather than holding the release indefinitely. The cited 2008 and 2021 descriptions are operating patterns, not universal deadlines or proof that all features are developed within one cycle. - **inferred**: Unnamed — pipeline review and integration. Put worthwhile changes into an integration tree where testing and development can proceed asynchronously. Require prior review, but judge a proposed review rule by whether it produces useful scrutiny rather than a count that can be gamed. At top-level integration, inspect the rationale and history, rely on the subsystem maintainers, and investigate overlaps or conflicts as evidence that independently developed changes may interact. Between merges Torvalds reports compiling the kernel with every module he can; that build check is useful coverage, not a substitute for runtime and hardware testing. - **inferred**: Unnamed — trusted maintainer network. Route each issue to people who know the subsystem, and let trusted maintainers integrate work from contributors they know. Evaluate a prospective maintainer by patch quality, responses to others, demonstrated judgment and sustained commitment; trust requires accepting some decisions one would not personally make. Maintainership is not permanent exclusive ownership: active contributors can cross boundaries after discussion. Use this when a central reviewer has become the bottleneck, while deliberately spreading specialized knowledge so one overworked person is not the only continuity plan. - **inferred**: Unnamed — system-boundary cost analysis. For a proposed split, compare complete systems, not a simplified component against the original whole. List required crossings, state exports, coherence rules, cross-domain races, deadlocks and debugging paths; locate the boundary where the interface requirements are simplest. Shared layering can still be valuable: a common VFS cache can centralize coherence and global replacement instead of repeating them in every filesystem. Torvalds invited practical evidence against his microkernel skepticism and corrected an earlier TLB example, so treat the analysis as a challenge to test, not a proof that every split is bad. - **inferred**: Unnamed — performance mechanism before benchmark conclusion. Identify the actual workload and critical resources, then explain why the proposed optimization should help: cache residency for a lookup table, branch predictability and dependency chains for conditional moves, or instruction-level parallelism for a timing loop. Test with representative competing data and on the relevant processor. A predictable branch on an aggressive out-of-order CPU can outperform a conditional move; an unpredictable branch or different processor can reverse that judgment. His 2003 and 2007 examples are mechanisms and historical assessments, not current benchmark results. - **inferred**: Unnamed — concurrency ordering audit. Enumerate events and the ordering constraints that each architecture actually guarantees; test a candidate execution against those constraints before declaring it impossible. Compare the full cost of lock-free alternatives, including barriers and correctness review, with an established lock; larger-than-word accesses may require a lock or a sequence-number approach with barriers. Prefer an existing synchronization primitive when its semantics express the ordering, as in his proposed parent-child suspend/resume scheme. His Alpha analysis was explicitly tentative and distinguished a theoretically permitted outcome from a known practical implementation. - **inferred**: Unnamed — code readability and churn review. Ask whether the local expression communicates its actual meaning: a boolean test can be implicit, but a negative error value should be recognizable as an error and its code should remain available. Reduce deep nesting with smaller functions or helpers rather than merely wrapping lines. Match surrounding shared-code style; allow more latitude in a specialized driver where its future maintainer bears the cost. Separate code movement from cleanup so reviewers can verify behavior and history tools have a better chance of following it. His suggested long-line window was a tentative signal, not a hard standard. - **inferred**: Unnamed — compatibility-led internal evolution. Separate external behavior from internal implementation. Preserve existing user-visible semantics, potentially with a compatibility layer, while changing internal subsystems incrementally in a unified tree. Before adding a new syscall or replacing an old one, identify real application demand and the continuing cost of supporting both semantics. A clean rewrite does not remove the need to support existing binaries and architectures. This approach does not mean preserving every out-of-tree driver interface or every obsolete hardware target indefinitely. - **inferred**: Unnamed — distributed version-control fit test. Require a repository model that supports independent work, data integrity and performance sufficient for frequent integration. Measure the actual workflow: Torvalds described an original Git target of applying 50–100 patches in roughly half a minute, not a general service-level objective. Local repositories permit private experiments and later sharing without first obtaining central write access; they do not eliminate social coordination. For large independent components, consider separate repositories and a superproject, while accounting for migration and tooling costs. Centralized systems can still work in tightly controlled organizations. - **inferred**: Unnamed — directed variation and selection. Start with a useful shared foundation, invite locally directed improvements from people with different needs, expose the results to users, and retain changes that work in context. Use this when the eventual shape of a general-purpose system is hard to predict. Selection matters as much as trying ideas: change itself is not automatically valuable, and a stable system in a stable environment may reasonably remain stable. The biological analogy is Torvalds's model of development, not an established law or a reason to accept unreviewed patches. - **inferred**: Unnamed — hardware-support and porting assessment. Before promising a port, establish access to interested developers, hardware and usable technical documentation; the CPU itself may be less obstructive than peripheral drivers and undocumented devices. For existing support, weigh real users against the recurring burden imposed on unrelated development, rather than treating age alone as the removal criterion. His PowerPC expectations were historical forecasts, and his later support judgment excluded museum-only use from his practical rationale. Neither supplies a numerical deprecation threshold. - **inferred**: Unnamed — accountable AI-assisted contribution. A human should validate a generated bug report, understand the affected code, propose a fix where appropriate and remain available for discussion. Distinguish a useful local patch from a symptom-level bandage that leaves the underlying design problem intact. AI review can surface questions and defects, but it can also produce poor reviews or plausible false reports that consume scarce maintainer time. Toy prototyping has a different acceptance bar from code that must be understood and maintained for years. - **inferred**: Unnamed — language and safety experiment. Identify the specific kernel work and its failure modes before choosing a language. Compare C's familiar low-level model and existing checking infrastructure with Rust's potential benefits and the added complexity of integration; test proposed uses in drivers or other bounded areas rather than assuming a wholesale replacement. In 2016 Torvalds doubted that language choice solved the hardest kernel problems; in 2021 he favored evaluating Rust as an augmentation, and in 2024 considered it too early to call the experiment a failure. None of these statements establishes a measured safety comparison.
