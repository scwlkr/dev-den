# Robert C. Martin (Uncle Bob): stories

Built from 129 accepted distinct works and 527,818 source words. Compiled reasoning with source support and limits below.

- **documented**: Kent Beck pairing experiment: Martin recalls initially dismissing test-first development in 1999. Pairing with Beck, he saw a small application grow through very short test and implementation steps without their needing a debugger during the roughly two-hour exercise. He then practiced for months before becoming reasonably proficient. The operational lesson is to try the discipline at its intended granularity before judging its feedback loop. The episode is a personal conversion, not proof that TDD eliminates debugging in larger systems. - **documented**: FitNesse persistence: Martin recounts building FitNesse with his son and others while deferring a planned MySQL decision. The team first used a mock, then in-memory pages, and finally flat files; he credits Michael Feathers with the flat-file implementation. That proved sufficient for their own needs and made test pages convenient to keep under version control. A customer later added a MySQL implementation behind the page abstraction. The case supports buying information through incremental decisions, not assuming flat files suit every persistence workload. - **documented**: FitNesse test symmetry: Martin reports that he once praised a one-to-one correspondence between production classes and test classes. As the project evolved, that structure contributed to fragile tests and resistance to design changes; later code reduced the correspondence. He learned to design tests as clients of behavioral APIs rather than mirror internal class boundaries. The case does not establish that every direct class test is harmful: the diagnostic is whether harmless internal changes repeatedly force widespread test edits. - **documented**: Immutable game duplication: In 2021 Martin traced a crash in his own game despite disciplined TDD and coverage. Two attackers independently produced updated versions of the same victim base, and reconstruction inserted both. An existing test checked theft but not the invariant that only one base remained. He added the missing assertion and used regression tests while replacing the algorithm. The lesson is to test conservation and identity invariants in immutable reconstruction; the case also limits claims that clean code or coverage alone prevents serious bugs. - **documented**: REPL-driven statistics program: Martin initially found interactive Clojure development fast because he could experiment against real data without constructing doubles. Later, direct file access made a structural change hard to verify; he delayed the refactoring and a deployed bug surfaced four days later. He returned to TDD and concluded that isolated inputs and outputs would have encouraged the decoupling he subsequently needed. This is one project retrospective, not a controlled comparison or a reason to avoid REPL exploration. - **documented**: Word-wrap estimation: Martin estimated a wrapping exercise at 10–15 minutes, then paired test-first with Kent Beck for 30 minutes without solving it before they had to teach. He observed that manually wrapping text is easy partly because a person can inspect and adjust the result without first specifying an algorithm. For estimation, enumerate procedural cases such as long words and boundary spaces before promising a duration. The anecdote illustrates uncertainty in a small task; it does not measure TDD against other methods. - **documented**: Dijkstra exercise: In a 2016 test-driven walkthrough, Martin found that a change making a two-edge example pass broke earlier degenerate cases. Later branching and parallel-path examples exposed further weaknesses and drove a more general approach that tracks reached nodes and path lengths. He called the progression jerky and said the result needed cleanup and was inefficient. The useful lesson is to run the existing suite and choose examples that expose algorithmic limitations; the demonstration is not a production-ready implementation. - **documented**: Timing-sensitive recommendation cache: Martin describes a team wrapping a slow recommendation calculation so callers receive a cached result while a refresh can run on another thread after an interval. Long test names became hard to interpret, and the team added a timing diagram and explanation to the test module. This qualified his usual reluctance to use comments: when timing relationships and rationale cannot be communicated clearly through names alone, an explanatory artifact can help. The account does not provide enough detail to validate the concurrency implementation. - **documented**: Rails service change propagation: In a 2012 case, Martin reports developers needing coordinated frontend, service-interface, and service MVC changes for a feature even when the database schema did not change. He diagnosed a partition organized around data management rather than families of business rules. First ask whether distribution is needed; if it is, consider boundaries around independently changing business capabilities, or more generic interfaces when physical separation is unavoidable. This diagnosis is his interpretation of the reported system, not a universal service decomposition. - **documented**: Clojure relay extraction: In 2023 Martin found that his messaging client mixed protocol rules with WebSocket handling. He extracted a relay abstraction around a URL, connection, callback, and operations such as open, close, and send; state updates returned new relay values. He also criticized his own tendency to accumulate Clojure functions without sufficient attention to class-like cohesion. The case shows that functional immutability does not remove the need to partition responsibilities; it is one design experience, not a comparative benchmark. - **documented**: A one-shot visual program: Martin reports writing a roughly 100-line visual-effects program without automated tests because it was disposable and almost entirely GUI work. He used a Clojure REPL and immediate visual inspection instead. In a separate interview he described accepting the risk of a small untested program while planning to add tests before refactoring it. The transferable decision is to assess lifetime, behavior, and consequences explicitly; these examples do not justify leaving important long-lived logic unverified. - **documented**: Agent architecture experiments: Martin reports that early agents produced messy structures and sometimes slowed down or broke other behavior as work continued. He manually redesigned module boundaries, built an architecture viewer, and added a deterministic dependency checker; automating architectural design itself had not worked well for him. In a later account of his Swarm Forge experiments, he preferred interacting with one agent over a heavily constrained harness for his tasks. Both are provisional personal observations, and neither establishes the best workflow for larger or safety-critical systems.
