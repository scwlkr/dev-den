# Robert C. Martin (Uncle Bob): positions

Built from 129 accepted distinct works and 527,818 source words. Compiled reasoning with source support and limits below.

- **documented**: Readable code: Martin treats code as communication to future programmers as well as instructions to a machine. Expressive names and small, cohesive functions help readers understand an operation without absorbing every detail. His preference is not a line-count law: in a 2024 interview he described four to six lines as common in his work but said a ten-line function is not automatically wrong. If extraction makes the reader navigate excessively, inline the pieces, understand the whole, and repartition them more clearly. - **documented**: Function structure: Keep statements at a consistent abstraction level, roughly one level below the function name, so the reader does not repeatedly jump from business concepts to string-buffer mechanics. Martin’s practical test for doing one thing is whether another meaningfully named function can be extracted. This is a judgment about communication, not a command to extract every expression. He also prefers few arguments, using roughly three as a soft limit, and suggests grouping several genuinely cohesive arguments. - **documented**: Comments and reader context: Martin normally treats comments as a last resort after trying to make code clear. In 2017, however, he documented a timing-sensitive design where a diagram and explanation communicated intent better than increasingly long test names. In a 2025 discussion he and John Ousterhout still disagreed over how much background a reader can be assumed to have. In a later agent-work interview, Martin allowed agents more comments because he observed them reading and maintaining those comments; that is a reader-specific adjustment, not a reversal into indiscriminate commenting. - **documented**: TDD cycle: His 2014 account prescribes a failing test before production code, only enough test to fail, and only enough implementation to pass. Refactor after the behavior works, using frequent feedback to limit the amount of unverified work. In 2016 he argued that short cycle size may matter more than test-first ordering itself; he also accepted a mutation-verified test-after suite as potentially professional. Later he described multiple disciplined alternatives and a possible preference for small test-after bundles in AI-assisted work. - **documented**: Testing and refactoring: The major payoff Martin assigns a test suite is confidence to make small structural changes without fear of unnoticed regressions. That payoff depends on both trust and speed. In 2014 he said he would accept an integration-test-centered approach if it met both conditions, even though he doubted many such suites would. A passing suite is evidence, not mathematical proof: missing assertions or unimagined requirements can still conceal failure. - **documented**: Coverage and mutation: Martin aims asymptotically toward complete line and branch coverage rather than declaring a lower percentage sufficient. He also recognizes that coverage shows execution, not whether assertions detect incorrect behavior, and that exhaustive path coverage is impractical. Mutation testing supplies another signal by changing code and checking whether tests notice. Investigate surviving mutations, but do not assume every survivor can or should be killed; neither a coverage number nor a mutation score establishes that the intended requirements are complete. ([unclebob-d8d717bc7e5d41](https://blog.cleancoder.com/uncle-bob/2016/06/10/MutationTesting.html) / evidence unclebob-d8d717bc7e5d41-e299bfbce3a6; [unclebob-2a1ed4dc2c938c](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5kCISBJwoZo&t=451) / evidence unclebob-2a1ed4dc2c938c-0149b9dbcb87; [unclebob-09830b9aa30a68](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2dKZ-dWaCiU&t=3757) / evidence unclebob-09830b9aa30a68-a31dc9b861b5; 3 independent supporting works)

- **documented**: Test design and doubles: Martin rejects both testing everything through slow infrastructure and mocking every collaborator. In his 2014 heuristic, use doubles chiefly across significant boundaries such as a database, server, or external service; retain some tests of the actual integration. Extensive spies and one-test-class-per-production-class structures can make behavior-preserving changes break tests. He commonly writes named doubles himself, while allowing tools when their capabilities are needed and exceptions when a specific test warrants them. ([unclebob-e89cc75a9cec9e](https://blog.cleancoder.com/uncle-bob/2014/05/10/WhenToMock.html) / evidence unclebob-e89cc75a9cec9e-25756cb0546a; [unclebob-e89cc75a9cec9e](https://blog.cleancoder.com/uncle-bob/2014/05/10/WhenToMock.html) / evidence unclebob-e89cc75a9cec9e-c7139fecc5a9; [unclebob-860320a823aea1](https://blog.cleancoder.com/uncle-bob/2014/05/14/TheLittleMocker.html) / evidence unclebob-860320a823aea1-3c3f78076bc9; [unclebob-110b06aa627af3](https://blog.cleancoder.com/uncle-bob/2017/10/03/TestContravariance.html) / evidence unclebob-110b06aa627af3-4d71093a421f; 3 independent supporting works)

- **documented**: Testing physical and visual boundaries: Martin does not apply test-first uniformly to CSS adjustment, physical devices, or trivial presentation glue. A mocked driver can establish that software sent a signal, not that a bell physically rang. His Humble Object approach moves significant decisions into testable logic and keeps the hard-to-automate edge thin; visual inspection or physical sensing checks what remains. This exception should not become a reason to leave complex GUI or device logic untested. - **documented**: Architecture and dependency direction: Clean Architecture protects higher-level policy by making source dependencies cross boundaries inward, even when runtime control or data flows outward. An inner output interface can be implemented by an outer presenter; a gateway can similarly shield business rules from persistence. Martin’s 2012 description says four circles are illustrative rather than mandatory. In a 2021 interview he said he does not diligently apply the full architecture to applications below roughly 5,000 lines, while a small component inside a larger system must still respect its containing boundaries. - **documented**: Use cases and presentation: Martin wants the high-level organization to reveal what an application does, not merely its framework. An interactor coordinates a specific use case; entities contain more general business rules. Plain request and response data allow direct tests without a web server. A presenter converts response data into display-ready strings and flags, leaving the view thin. This is a way to protect useful separation, not a requirement that every small program create the same set of classes. - **documented**: Frameworks and storage: Frameworks and databases can save substantial work, but their conventions and evolution can pull business policy into implementation details. Martin prefers interfaces and translation at significant boundaries, keeping framework inheritance, SQL, and ORM concerns outside the core. He acknowledges the immediate cost of mapping and says it may not pay for a disposable month-long application. Replacement remains possible rather than necessarily easy; isolation also matters when the infrastructure is never replaced but changes independently of business rules. - **documented**: Deferred decisions versus upfront design: Martin favors postponing consequential infrastructure commitments until working use cases provide better information, but does not equate deferral with postponing every UI or database experiment. He also rejects the idea that TDD creates a complete architecture automatically. Sketch a provisional system shape and its important boundaries, then revise it when code and tests expose mistakes. His 2014 distinction is that detailed object design is risky upfront, while disentangling major architectural boundaries later can be expensive. - **documented**: Single responsibility and change: SRP is not a one-method-per-class rule. Martin’s 2014 Employee example separates pay calculation, hours reporting, and persistence because finance, operations, and technology can request changes for different reasons. Group elements that change together and separate those that change independently, so a change for one stakeholder is less likely to disturb another. The useful boundary depends on actual responsibilities and change patterns; a microservice that mixes those reasons remains tangled despite its network boundary. ([unclebob-9155454a988921](https://blog.cleancoder.com/uncle-bob/2014/05/08/SingleReponsibilityPrinciple.html) / evidence unclebob-9155454a988921-423c4b36d54f; [unclebob-9155454a988921](https://blog.cleancoder.com/uncle-bob/2014/05/08/SingleReponsibilityPrinciple.html) / evidence unclebob-9155454a988921-8038adb1deca; [unclebob-65ca06070f1683](https://blog.cleancoder.com/uncle-bob/2020/10/18/Solid-Relevance.html) / evidence unclebob-65ca06070f1683-50c18d626f06; 2 independent supporting works)

- **documented**: Open-closed principle: Martin’s earlier wording suggested closed modules were not to be modified; in 2013 he explicitly called that absolute formulation overstated. No significant system can be closed against every change. Instead, protect selected, observed or important axes of variation so new behavior can often be added with limited changes to existing code. A polymorphic shape design may ease adding shapes but still need revision when the customer asks for a new drawing order. Abstractions have costs, so choose them strategically. - **documented**: Substitution and interface segregation: A substitute must preserve what its clients reasonably rely on, not merely fit a taxonomic inheritance relationship. Martin’s mutable square breaks a rectangle client that expects changing height to leave width alone. In 2020 he corrected his own earlier tendency to view substitution as only an inheritance issue: interfaces and duck types also carry behavioral expectations. Keep interfaces focused on client needs, but avoid creating so many client- or version-specific interfaces that their maintenance becomes unmanageable. - **documented**: Switches, polymorphism, and data: Repeated switches over the same variant can scatter changes and create missed cases. A factory can localize variant selection and policy can use an interface, but Martin does not apply this mechanically: in 2021 he preferred a simpler refactoring when the requirement was only a translation. His objects-versus-data analysis also makes the axis of change explicit: polymorphic classes ease adding types, while operations over discriminated data ease adding operations. The better representation depends on which change is expected. - **documented**: Microservices: Martin treats services as a deployment choice, not an architecture by themselves. They can offer independent restart, deployment, and technology choices, but introduce communication, configuration, coordination, and message-versioning costs. His 2014 recommendation was to start with decoupled packages or plugins and distribute selectively when a real need arises. A network boundary does not itself enforce clean data ownership: a service can still exchange a giant shared blob. Scalability, failover, or security may justify accepting service costs. - **documented**: Functional and object-oriented programming: Martin regards the techniques as compatible, not competing eras. In his deliberately reductive account, functional programming constrains assignment while object-oriented polymorphism helps reverse source dependencies. Immutable data eliminates races caused by concurrent updates to that data, but does not eliminate every concurrency or correctness problem. He previously expected rapid laptop core-count growth and later questioned that forecast while retaining his interest in functional programming. Clojure protocols show how immutable code can still use gateway polymorphism. - **documented**: Types and contracts: Martin’s history moved from favoring assembler and finding Pascal restrictive, through embracing C++ after C type errors, to preferring dynamically typed Clojure. In 2017 he acknowledged that static checks can catch representation errors tests might miss, even while arguing that behavioral tests remain necessary and type constraints have notation and change costs. By 2021 he favored programmer-controlled Clojure specifications and postconditions. This is his trade-off judgment, not proof that runtime checks and comprehensive tests provide the same guarantees as static types. - **documented**: Performance and extraction: Martin generally prefers making code work, cleaning it with tests, then measuring before optimizing hot spots. He nevertheless recognizes a real-time exception: function extraction can impose unacceptable time or stack costs in constrained systems. His proposed response is to extract first, measure deadlines, and undo selected extractions where the measured cost is unacceptable. This is a practical sequence for his example, not evidence that performance constraints can always safely be deferred. - **documented**: Agile planning: Martin describes Agile as especially suited to small teams working in short iterations. Measure completed work, use the observed rate to forecast, communicate bad news early, and negotiate scope when the forecast misses the desired date. He personally prefers one-week iterations but acknowledges they are harder to adopt. Estimates are guesses, not promises; his three-point example uses optimistic, nominal, and pessimistic outcomes. He says Agile improves visibility rather than automatically making a team faster. - **documented**: Pairing: Martin values pairing especially for sharing system knowledge and covering for absent colleagues, not as an all-day mandate. In 2021 he estimated that its strongest value lies in nontrivial work of moderate depth; trivial work may not justify it, and genuinely deep problems may require uninterrupted concentration.
