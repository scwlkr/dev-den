- **documented**: School-based database partitioning, described in 2005. Zuckerberg explained that early Facebook used separate MySQL instances by school because he estimated most activity was local. That reduced the graph traversal needed for common operations compared with searching the full network. His role was explaining an architectural choice made by the early company, not presenting a benchmarked general sharding algorithm. The transferable lesson is to partition around measured access locality; the limit is that cross-school relationships and later usage patterns can erode that assumption. - **documented**: Web and database separation, described in 2005. Zuckerberg recounted moving from Apache and MySQL on the same machines to separate database servers and a load-balanced web pool. The change addressed uneven traffic among schools and reduced the coupling that had made a shared-server failure stop page loading. His account illustrates matching deployment boundaries to workload and failure modes. It does not establish that separation alone provided high availability, or that the historical topology fits modern infrastructure. - **inferred**: Cache speed versus failure load, described in 2005. Zuckerberg contrasted slower database queries with much faster cached accesses, but noted that cache-node failures generated misses that concentrated demand on databases. His latency figures are historical estimates, not planning targets. - **documented**: Photo upload bottleneck, described in 2005. When a router upgrade was infeasible before launch, Zuckerberg said Facebook compressed photos on the client with a Java applet and ActiveX control. The primary benefit was reducing upload time across a network bottleneck; saved server CPU was secondary. His role was to explain the team's practical workaround. The lesson is to locate the constrained resource before buying or optimizing a different one. The specific client technologies are historical and should not be copied as a current implementation recommendation. - **documented**: Open Compute and ecosystem economics. In 2024 Zuckerberg argued that sharing server, networking and data-center designs helped create a common supply chain, greater volume and lower costs, which he said saved Meta billions. He distinguished this infrastructure case from releasing differentiated product code. His role was explaining Meta's strategic rationale, not independently auditing the savings.
