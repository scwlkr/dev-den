# John Carmack: stories

Built from 24 accepted distinct works and 471,558 source words. Compiled reasoning with source support and limits below.

- **documented**: Quake 2 server saturation, 1998. Carmack investigated a server that had wedged at full CPU utilization. Profiling attributed 60% of execution time to client movement simulation. Making that component thread-safe appeared feasible, but concurrent client commands implied substantial changes to the server and game DLL; multiple independent servers could host more players per machine than one large parallel server. Neither proposed optimization was immediately pursued. The operational lesson is to profile first and compare the desired outcome with simpler deployment alternatives. The 60% figure describes that server and workload, not all multiplayer games. - **documented**: Generic OpenGL threading, 1998–1999. Carmack built a generic producer-consumer wrapper expecting two processors to overlap rendering work. It initially ran 20% slower; measurements showed about 12 ms to fill and 17 ms to drain approximately 1.5 MB of commands per frame. Copying and cache traffic consumed the expected benefit. He abandoned the generic wrapper and later used renderer-level double-buffered data instead; on one 1999 machine the revised SMP path improved an average timedemo by about 35%, with larger gains in some scenes. The lesson is to account for communication and measure the replacement, while recognizing driver and workload dependence. - **documented**: QuakeC-to-C port, 1998. Carmack explained repeated Quake 2 instability as a consequence of transferring interpreted game logic directly into C. Old defects that the interpreter's safety conventions had made harmless became dangerous under the new execution model. He concluded that the team should have redesigned the code around C's strengths and weaknesses instead of patching one crash after another. The transferable question is which invariants the old runtime provided implicitly and which the new environment no longer enforces. The account does not establish that every subsequent crash shared this cause. ([carmack-0dc84858363862](https://www.fabiensanglard.net/fd_proxy/doom3/pdfs/johnc-plan_1998.pdf) / evidence carmack-0dc84858363862-40f640dfc025; 1 independent supporting works)

- **documented**: Quake III virtual machine, 1998–1999. Carmack moved away from an earlier assumption that Java was the right mod language and designed a portable sandbox for compiled C. A proposed shortcut allowing VM reads from host address space looked convenient, but Gregory Maxwell raised a 64-bit pointer portability problem; Carmack rejected it and accepted more work. He initially wanted higher-level interpreter optimizations before native generation, then changed course when performance missed its target: one test measured 52.9 native, 50.2 compiled bytecode and 43.9 interpreted, with generated code roughly five times bytecode size. The result illustrates revising implementation choices against measurements without treating the sandbox claim as an external security audit. - **documented**: Misdiagnosed Quake III network trouble, 1999. Players reported networking problems in a newer test build. Carmack compared builds under controlled conditions and found that systems with insufficient memory were swapping; with enough memory, the networking was identical in that comparison. This is a useful pattern for debugging a symptom that points toward the wrong subsystem: hold the workload constant, vary the suspected resource and check competing explanations before rewriting the network stack. The result explains the tested report, not every case of network-like stutter. - **documented**: Doom 3 unified replay strategy. Carmack attempted to use synchronous execution as a common foundation for saves, demos and networking. Consistency problems and slow replay-based loading defeated the plan, and separate solutions had to be created late. He called it an early strategic mistake while still considering it a potentially worthwhile experiment. The lesson is to establish a realistic end-to-end proof and a fallback before several shipping requirements depend on one elegant mechanism. His earlier reflection on partial packet journaling also shows that reproducibility had been a longstanding unresolved problem, not a trivial oversight. - **documented**: Rage virtual-texture budgets. Carmack's early capacity estimate assumed a 4K cache had ample room relative to the screen. Production art instead contained many disconnected texture islands: a tiny visible railing could require a full 128-by-128 page. He also judged it a bad call to encourage ambitious content without giving artists concrete limits, because the resulting scenes demanded extensive engineering. The practical lesson is to test cache granularity and budgets with representative production assets, not only continuous terrain-like demos. The case does not imply that artists alone caused the mismatch or that one universal budget number would have sufficed. - **documented**: Rage PC launch and drivers. Carmack accepted responsibility for engineering toward corrected drivers while insufficiently accounting for customers who would not have them, even before a driver-release failure. He also noted that Rage's unusual continuous texture uploads exercised less-common driver paths. Tests and internal analysis could pass while the complete customer system still failed. The lesson is to test realistic combinations of deployed drivers and installation conditions, and to treat external dependency behavior as part of delivery risk. His account is a retrospective explanation, not an independently established attribution of every launch failure. - **documented**: Static analysis changed his debugging practice. Carmack had abandoned an early noisy PC-Lint trial, but later tools found serious defects in code he had thought comparatively clean. One multi-day debugging investigation led back to an analyzer warning that had not yet been fixed. He enabled analysis in ordinary builds and advocated using multiple tools, while acknowledging false positives and an unresolved backlog. The operational lesson is to make useful feedback immediate and actionable, then budget explicitly for warning triage. A warning is evidence to investigate, not proof that every flagged path is defective. - **documented**: Android VR latency and a partner demonstration. In an early mobile VR experiment, replacing a sensor did not solve the major delay: Carmack found that Android's default triple buffering could add up to roughly 48 ms before scan-out. He used a device-specific buffer-chain hack to demonstrate front-buffer rendering, making the difference directly experienceable; Samsung then supplied a supported interface. The lesson is to measure the whole motion-to-display path and use a working comparison to resolve a platform disagreement. The hack worked on one phone, and tightly scheduled front-buffer rendering introduces its own deadline risks. - **documented**: Mobile clock scaling and missed frames. Carmack noticed that touching the screen unexpectedly stabilized playback. Investigation led to Android power management: sleeping between frames looked like spare capacity, so clocks fell, and missed frames could further worsen the interaction. Fixed clock levels provided a known budget for testing, but a mobile VR application still needed to run within sustained thermal limits. The lesson is to examine feedback loops between the application and platform controller, not just the application's isolated frame time. Fixed peak clocks are not a guarantee of safe sustained operation. - **documented**: Valve's low-persistence prototype. Carmack had expected a sufficiently fast OLED display to solve VR motion blur. A prototype with directly adjustable persistence convinced him that sample-and-hold behavior still mattered, and he publicly retracted the earlier assessment. The useful method is to make the disputed variable controllable and compare the experience directly, rather than debating component specifications alone. His confidence was stronger for head-mounted viewing than for conventional gameplay; the demonstration does not establish a universal preferred persistence value. - **documented**: Minecraft VR movement and comfort. Carmack tried stuttering detected accelerations to reduce sickness, but found the resulting movement unpleasant. Buffering and linearizing motion helped at the cost of input latency. He then exploited deterministic jump and fall trajectories, using a small lookup table to remap common step movements without the large generic buffering delay. This is a domain-specific escape from a general trade-off, not a complete solution for every acceleration or every user. A separate swivel-chair test with a motion-sensitive colleague provided encouraging viability evidence, but one tolerable session did not prove universal comfort. - **documented**: Armadillo's iteration bottleneck. Carmack concluded that contract work kept the organization operating without reliably advancing its own suborbital objective. He stopped the contracts and funded focused development, but the expected two-year plan still did not deliver an integrated successful mission. In hindsight he identified failure to build multiple vehicles or spare assemblies as a critical mistake: crashes halted learning instead of being absorbed by a modular fleet. He offered several possible explanations for the slowdown and explicitly could not isolate his own reduced involvement. Faster iteration is a useful design goal, not a substitute for safety-critical aerospace discipline.
