# John Carmack: stories

Built from 53 accepted distinct works and 730,256 source words. Compiled reasoning with source support and limits below.

- **documented**: QuakeWorld prediction, 1996. Carmack redesigned Internet play around unreliable packets and client-side prediction, moving away from a simpler terminal-like client. His initial approach replayed local movement from the latest authoritative update, but doors, projectiles and aiming still exposed network delay. Within days he reported that simulating 300 ms ahead caused serious inconsistencies in a fast action game and narrowed the ambition to shorter prediction for smoothing. The case teaches an advisor to test where reconciliation fails, rather than presenting prediction as latency removal. His own qualification matters: longer prediction might suit driving or flight better than Quake. - **documented**: Quake II's language transition, 1998. Carmack explained repeated instability after interpreted QuakeC logic was ported directly to C. Old defects that had been harmless under the interpreter's safety conventions became crashes under the new environment. He concluded that the team should have redesigned for C's strengths and weaknesses instead of fixing failures one at a time. For an advisor, the transferable test is to enumerate which assumptions the old runtime enforced before changing languages or execution models. The case does not establish that native C is inherently unsuitable, or that every crash shared one cause. ([carmack-0dc84858363862](https://www.fabiensanglard.net/fd_proxy/doom3/pdfs/johnc-plan_1998.pdf) / evidence carmack-0dc84858363862-40f640dfc025; [carmack-3cfb25e43ff7a1](https://github.com/ESWAT/john-carmack-plan-archive/blob/master/by_year/johnc_plan_1997.txt) / evidence carmack-3cfb25e43ff7a1-6d68d22c29bd; 2 independent supporting works)

- **documented**: The OpenGL threading wrapper, 1998–1999. Carmack built a generic producer-consumer wrapper expecting dual-processor parallelism, but it initially ran 20 percent slower. Instrumentation showed roughly 12 ms filling and 17 ms draining about 1.5 MB of commands per frame; cache and memory traffic erased the anticipated gain. He abandoned the generic interception approach for explicit renderer threading with less communication and double-buffered data. Later measurements showed useful speedups in some scenes, but driver and platform reliability remained a concern. The lesson is not to avoid threading; compare end-to-end work, transfer costs and deployment stability with the serial baseline. - **documented**: Quake III's mod VM, 1998–1999. Carmack moved away from his earlier assumption that Java was the right mod language and proposed C compiled for a restricted virtual machine. The design isolated mod memory and system calls while retaining native DLLs for debugging or selected server-side use. When interpreter performance missed his target, he changed course again and implemented dynamic code generation: one test ran at 50.2 versus 52.9 native and 43.9 interpreted, with substantially larger generated code. The case demonstrates an explicit security, portability, speed and memory trade, not proof that the sandbox was invulnerable. - **documented**: The scissor-test assumption, 1999. Carmack had blamed a graphics driver for full-buffer clears, but investigating the actual path showed that scissor testing was not enabled by default. Enabling it made most cards he tested a few percent faster, and he acknowledged the mistake. The operational lesson is to inspect the state and produce a controlled comparison before assigning responsibility to another layer. The result is limited to those drivers and workloads; enabling a state is not a general optimization recommendation without checking semantics and measurement. - **documented**: Doom 3 audio simplification. After taking over audio code, Carmack removed sophisticated processing that designers frequently bypassed and substituted a smaller, predictable mixer with basic attenuation and localization. He reported improved robustness and happier sound designers. Later he identified head-tracked VR as a possible setting where more sophisticated spatial processing could be worth revisiting. Use the case to audit features against actual adoption and failure cost, not to conclude that audio sophistication is always wasteful or that sound design is unimportant. - **documented**: Rage's virtual-texture budget. Carmack's early cache estimate worked poorly when artists built fragmented modular scenes rather than large continuous surfaces. A tiny visible railing could consume an entire 128-by-128 page, and a single 4K cache image was insufficient. He also called it a bad decision not to give artists concrete limits when they asked. The production response introduced more complex paths and caches across memory and storage. The lesson is to test representative content and supply actionable budgets early; the exact cache dimensions and storage behavior were specific to Rage's platforms. - **documented**: Rage's PC launch. Carmack accepted responsibility for engineering toward corrected graphics drivers without adequately accounting for the customers who would not have them, even if the driver release had gone as planned. The actual launch also encountered driver availability and compatibility failures. Internal analysis and tests did not protect the whole customer experience from dependencies outside the application. For deployment advice, include realistic installed versions, update paths and fallback behavior in the release plan. The account identifies a serious integration failure; it does not establish that all PC drivers or unusual rendering paths are inevitably unreliable. - **documented**: Static-analysis conversion. Carmack had abandoned an earlier noisy PC-Lint trial, but later analyzers found serious defects in code that appeared warning-clean. One multiday debugging investigation led back to an analyzer warning he had not yet fixed. He integrated analysis into normal builds and used multiple tools, while admitting that thousands of noisy PC-lint warnings remained unreviewed. The applicable lesson is to improve signal, delivery and ownership of findings, not merely purchase an analyzer. Tool warnings are evidence to investigate; clean results from one tool do not establish absence of defects. - **documented**: Early headset sensor work, 2012. Rather than accept a disappointing headset as evidence that VR hardware was inadequate, Carmack decomposed the motion-to-display chain. He requested raw sensor values, adapted orientation-integration work from Armadillo and obtained a firmware update that increased the sensor update rate from 125 to 250 Hz. He estimated that sensor changes removed about 85 ms from the loop, while display processing and sensor drift remained separate problems. The lesson is to measure and replace the responsible stage, not assume the newest component is the bottleneck. These figures describe his test setup, not every headset. - **documented**: Valve's low-persistence demonstration. Carmack had expected a 120 Hz OLED display to be enough for VR motion clarity. Valve showed him an on/off comparison of low persistence, and he revised that assessment: pixels remaining illuminated during eye and head motion still produced visible smear. The useful decision pattern is to build an immediate A/B experience that tests the disputed perceptual assumption. It overturned his judgment for head-mounted displays; he was less certain how much the same change would matter in conventional gameplay. - **documented**: Mobile VR front-buffer proof, 2014. Carmack timed an Android rendering path and found default triple buffering could add roughly 48 ms before scanout, undermining the team's motion-to-photon goal. He developed a device-specific front-buffer proof of concept and showed the difference to Samsung; the company subsequently supplied a supported interface. Rendering ahead of scanout traded ordinary buffering simplicity for strict timing. The case supports using a measurable prototype to persuade a platform partner, but the original hack worked on one phone and should not be treated as a portable production recipe. - **documented**: Minecraft VR comfort experiment. Carmack tried stuttering detected accelerations to reduce sickness, but found the result unpleasant. Buffering movement into linear segments helped at the cost of input latency. He then exploited deterministic jump and fall trajectories with a small lookup table, producing useful step-ups and step-downs without comparable buffering. A motion-sensitive tester's tolerable 20-minute session improved confidence in the project's viability, but did not prove universal comfort. The lesson is to use domain-specific knowledge to escape a generic trade-off, then test both responsiveness and user variation. - **documented**: View-dependent VR video streaming. Carmack initially saw promise in spending bandwidth on the direction a viewer was looking, but long compression groups delayed quality changes after a head turn; shortening them reduced compression efficiency. In a later account, A/B observations showed viewers abandoning videos amid hitches and quality changes, making the approach a net negative despite excellent central detail at times. The decision rule is to assess adaptation latency and actual viewing behavior alongside peak image quality. The result applies to the implementation and tests described, not every possible adaptive streaming design. - **documented**: Keen's physical Atari setup, 2025. Carmack described an agent observing a real television through a camera and controlling a joystick with a robot. Physical timing exposed intermediate actions: firing could happen before the stick reached its intended direction, making action history important. Extracting reward from the displayed score also proved fragile under lighting, scanout and framing changes; fiducials were a partial compromise. Networks learned in place, but the team had not established reliable transfer to other locations. The case tests assumptions hidden by emulator interfaces; it is not evidence that physical Atari solves general robotics or AGI.
