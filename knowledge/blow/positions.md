# Jonathan Blow: positions

Built from 51 accepted distinct works and 321,859 source words. Compiled reasoning with source support and limits below.

- **documented**: C++ and migration. In 2014, Blow recognized why game programmers use C++: direct memory access and performance matter. His objection was the accumulated interaction of features and compatibility constraints, not that every C++ feature is bad. He thought a replacement needed a substantial practical advantage to justify switching. For an established engine, he proposed incremental migration through compatible calls, data structures, or replaceable libraries rather than a wholesale rewrite. - **documented**: Language design for real workloads. Blow wants a practical systems language shaped by recurring game-programming problems, not a universal answer to every programming style. In 2018 he described testing the language on a substantial commercial game; in 2021 he contrasted complex applications with toy examples. That does not establish that his choices suit every domain: defensive tools for inexperienced developers and different safety requirements can be legitimate goals. - **documented**: Safety versus experimentation. Blow values correctness and early error detection but disputes that mandatory restrictions are always the best route. His 2014 test was total completion cost: time satisfying constraints plus time finding and fixing bugs. In 2020 he praised Rust's correctness direction while worrying that mandatory friction could inhibit exploration; he explicitly lacked proof that his own approach was better. Runtime checks require the faulty path to execute and do not equal a sound static guarantee. - **documented**: Ownership and allocation strategy. In 2014 Blow sketched compiler-understood ownership on pointers as a possible aid, while acknowledging unresolved interactions with custom allocators. By the 2020 beta Q&A, he said he had removed a planned ownership feature after learning to use fewer independently managed allocations and collective temporary lifetimes. This is a change in his own design, not evidence that ownership checking has no value elsewhere. - **documented**: Temporary storage. Blow favors arenas for values that cannot outlive a known reset point: allocate cheaply and clear collectively, for example at a frame boundary. The essential correctness condition is that no consumer retains a temporary value past reset. His allocator later gained overflow-page handling and cleanup, sacrificing some original simplicity and theoretical speed for practical use. Collective lifetime management is not a substitute for proving that lifetime boundary. - **documented**: Context allocators. A shared context lets downstream code use a substituted allocator without rewriting each allocation call, and can also coordinate logging and assertion handling. In a 2025 demonstration, Blow showed allocator hooks for memory inspection, while noting that allocations hidden inside external APIs are not automatically visible. He also identified unresolved friction when context crosses separately compiled DLL boundaries. The benefit depends on participating code honoring the convention. - **documented**: Resource management and RAII. Blow criticizes uniform object-by-object resource management when it encourages scattered allocations, obscures important lifetime events, or fails to exploit memory-specific opportunities. He prefers explicit close timing when subsequent correctness depends on it. This is not an absolute rejection of scope-bound cleanup: in his profiler he used C++ construction and destruction to reliably leave timing zones on early returns. - **documented**: Entity lifetime. In his game architecture, long-lived references should use IDs or handles and handle lookup failure; direct entity pointers are limited to a known lifetime such as a frame. A project-specific metaprogram can reject pointers stored in non-whitelisted persistent structures. The check encodes a local engine invariant, not a general proof of memory safety. Custom allocators and entity systems can also hide logical lifetimes from a language's ordinary checks. - **documented**: Builds and dependency availability. Blow wants a program, its dependencies, build logic, and compiler to define a build without a fragile collection of platform-specific tools and network fetches. - **documented**: Compilation speed and iteration. Blow treats fast clean builds as a quality-of-life and experimentation feature, not merely a benchmark. A 2025 demonstration reported approximately 2.3 seconds for a clean build of roughly 300,000 lines; the number belongs to that configuration, not every Jai project. - **documented**: Compiler architecture. Blow favors stages and compilation units determined by actual dependencies rather than a rigid philosophical schema. - **documented**: Compile-time execution and trust. Blow favors powerful compile-time computation in the same language as runtime code, including operations with external effects. He acknowledges that this can make exploitation easier and that nondeterministic operations can undermine reproducible debugging. His decision prioritizes exploring utility for trusted teams; it is not a security guarantee or a recommendation to execute untrusted dependencies without isolation. - **documented**: Closures. Blow's earlier university-influenced expectation was to add closures early. By the 2020 beta Q&A, his practical work had made them less urgent, and he worried about separately managed captured data and hidden lifetime or allocation costs. He still considered them potentially useful. His tentative sequence was to introduce function values with code and data pointers first, study consequences, then build closures on that representation. - **inferred**: Abstraction and actual benefit. Blow assesses an abstraction by the work it removes, the important distinctions it hides, and the cost of its interactions. - **documented**: ECS and engine structure. Blow treats entity-component organization as a dial, not an identity that an entire engine must adopt. A general-purpose engine may need to accommodate unknown customers' data structures; a team building a specific engine may not have that problem. Compare candidate layouts by correctness, comprehensibility, code complexity, and runtime behavior. His objection to universal prescriptions does not establish that ECS is inherently slow or unsuitable. - **documented**: Data-oriented performance. Blow recommends examining actual data transformations and memory access, including cache misses and dependent pointer reads, rather than assuming an object hierarchy is the right performance model. He credits others for originating data-oriented design. The practical objection to individual-object lifetime machinery includes opportunity cost: it can discourage batching and contiguous processing, even when its immediate instruction overhead appears small. - **documented**: Profiling and optimization order. Blow wants profiling to explain behavior, including invisible correctness mistakes such as drawing twice or testing too many distant collisions. - **documented**: Frame consistency. Average throughput is insufficient when occasional long frames affect player control. Blow links missed inputs and stutter to fairness in action games and to trust and precision even in puzzle games. He cautions that persistent frame-coherence schemes can create feedback between frame time and work, though some systems such as physics may make coherence hard to avoid. Choose measurements and budgets for the actual interaction. - **documented**: Optimization stopping point. Blow advocates understanding hardware and then stopping when a subsystem is sufficiently fast for the product. He describes learning SIMD to improve Braid particles and moving on, while recognizing he could probably improve that implementation with later experience. - **documented**: Quantization. Choose a representation using application invariants, not average error alone. Center reconstruction reduces truncation bias under stated assumptions, but cannot represent zero and can make a supposedly stationary saved object creep. Blow recommends rounded endpoint reconstruction as a robust default when preserving zero and one matters, accepting an extra interval. Nonuniform input distributions can justify different interval sizes, subject to worst-case requirements. - **documented**: Network compression and security. Compression must match delivery semantics: ordinary adaptive models can diverge when packets are lost or reordered. Blow describes a receiver-directed model-update protocol, but its sample has explicit storage and sequence-number limitations.
