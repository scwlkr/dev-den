# Vitalik Buterin: principles

Built from 57 accepted distinct works and 250,874 source words. Compiled reasoning with source support and limits below.

- **inferred**: Protect independent verification when scaling. High confidence. Before increasing capacity, measure whether a critical mass can still run verifying software on realistic hardware and connections, including catch-up and attack margins. The 2014 concern was concentration among large full-node operators; the 2021 analysis quantified interacting CPU, storage, and bandwidth constraints; the 2024 Verge proposes reducing verification costs. Proofs may change the bottleneck, but data download and censorship remain. Do not treat advertised throughput as the whole objective. - **inferred**: Separate a proof's guarantee from the assumptions that make it useful. High confidence. State timing, honest-participant, availability, cryptographic, and implementation assumptions alongside each claimed guarantee. A formal reduction does not prove its hardness assumption or human behavior, and a proof of execution does not establish that users can obtain the data needed to exit. Review out-of-model incentives and operational failure paths as well as the theorem. This is not a rejection of formal methods; it is a requirement to specify their boundaries. - **inferred**: Price the adversarial worst case, not just average use. High confidence. Resource prices and limits should reflect the maximum work a malicious block or transaction can force on validators and provers. The original gas model meters execution; the 2024 multidimensional proposal distinguishes state access from other resources; the Verge warns that an attacker could construct blocks that clog provers for minutes. Keep average targets and hard per-block constraints conceptually separate, and validate actual client behavior at maximum size. - **inferred**: Design for correlated control, not nominally separate keys or nodes. High confidence. A 2014 example shows how a web wallet provider controlling one key and the interface can effectively capture another in a 2-of-3 arrangement. Likewise, multiple clients or provers add less safety when they share failure causes. Separate software, operators, custody, and verification paths where practical, and examine defaults and hosting dependencies. Independence is an operational property to establish, not a number of boxes on an architecture diagram. Corroboration: high (3 distinct works; this measures source count, not truth). - **inferred**: Make security incentives specific to the actor and attack. High confidence. An individual stakeholder may benefit from signing both forks even if the currency's collective integrity is valuable; a custodian's incentives can differ from those of the owner. Slash provable equivocation where the protocol can identify it, but treat censorship separately because absence is harder to prove. Test whether challengers are paid, altruistic, or merely assumed to exist. Economic security is not established by saying participants have a general interest in the network. - **inferred**: Keep consensus responsibilities narrow. High confidence. External price judgments, application bailouts, and additional validator duties can turn disputes into base-layer governance crises and encourage outsourcing. Let applications choose oracle and recovery mechanisms; allow dual use of stake when losses remain with participants rather than recruiting Ethereum social consensus for rescue. This does not forbid shared security or every protocol-level feature: it asks whether the failure is contained and whether validators can reasonably carry the new obligation. - **inferred**: Treat exit rights as capacity and timing requirements. High confidence. A nominal right to exit is weak if data is missing, fees are unaffordable, or simultaneous exits cannot fit before the deadline. Plasma's seven-day example makes monitoring and challenges explicit; the 2025 calculations distinguish mass-exit throughput from price; rollup data availability supports reconstruction. Test the entire emergency path under correlated demand rather than demonstrating one successful withdrawal in normal conditions. - **inferred**: Preserve options without disguising interim risk. High confidence. - **inferred**: Differentiate safety, liveness, latency, and censorship. High confidence. A system can prevent invalid state yet freeze withdrawals, deliver fast preconfirmations without finality, or validate blocks while centralized producers suppress transactions. Set separate requirements and recovery paths for each. A higher finality quorum can cause more temporary non-finality while making some contentious attacks harder; short slots can exclude geographically distant validators. Do not collapse these properties into a single security score. Corroboration: high (3 distinct works; this measures source count, not truth). - **inferred**: Use cryptography to remove dependencies, not to pretend they vanished. High confidence. Compare concrete proof cost, setup assumptions, security maturity, and what the proof actually reveals. A multiparty setup can reduce trust to at least one honest contributor, while a validity proof does not itself publish data. Privacy tools also reveal different metadata or outputs; a blockchain alone provides authenticity rather than confidentiality. Choose a simpler or weaker construction when its remaining trust is acceptable and its costs are better understood. Corroboration: high (4 distinct works; this measures source count, not truth). - **inferred**: Revisit old assumptions when new evidence changes the attack model. Medium confidence. In July 2014 Buterin analyzed transactions-as-proof-of-stake as a proof of concept while acknowledging scalability and re-signing problems. In November he argued that users might profit from re-signing old transactions or selling obsolete keys, making the assumed near-zero long-range attack probability unstable. The operational lesson is to model strategic adaptation explicitly; do not carry the early tentative construction forward as his final position. - **documented**: Do not infer total cost from marginal incentives alone. Medium confidence. In his 2014 and 2017 proof-of-stake analyses, participants have different liquidity preferences, so a marginal reward sufficient to attract additional stake does not mean all participants incur that same cost. Stake lockup still has an opportunity cost, and borrowed or temporarily controlled coins may be the relevant attack resource. Compare real mechanisms, access to capital, and recovery costs rather than asserting that proof of stake must waste as much as proof of work.
