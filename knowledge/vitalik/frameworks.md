# Vitalik Buterin: frameworks

Built from 130 accepted distinct works and 522,002 source words. Compiled reasoning with source support and limits below.

- **documented**: Trust-model decomposition. First identify the property at stake: safety, liveness, privacy or recovery. Then list the required actors, whether any one of them can be replaced, their incentives, and what happens when the assumption fails. A 1-of-N fraud-proof system is materially different from trusting one named operator, but still requires an honest, capable and connected watcher. Use this when comparing bridges, rollups, oracles and service providers. Decision guidance: prefer the weakest adequate assumption for the most consequential failure, but do not optimize one component to zero trust while ignoring a more vulnerable dependency elsewhere. - **documented**: Scalability trilemma. Evaluate throughput, affordable independent participation, and security against a substantial adversarial share together. Next identify which work each node must perform: execution, state storage, transaction networking and data download. Simple large blocks increase capacity but raise node costs; independent chains divide security. Use the framework before accepting a throughput claim. In his 2021 illustration, resistance above 25% was acceptable while 5% was clearly insufficient; those figures are examples, not universal protocol thresholds. Sharding and proofs change the architecture rather than simply selecting two corners. - **documented**: Encapsulated versus systemic complexity. Separate complexity inside a component from complexity that crosses interfaces, incentives and operational assumptions. Compare the whole system, not specification length alone: a mathematically difficult proof system may hide complexity behind a simple verification interface, while a simple fraud-proof mechanism can require watchers, incentives, timely challenges and censorship defenses. Use this when choosing cryptographic primitives or protocol architectures. Decision guidance: moderately favor encapsulation when it removes many external caveats, but still count implementation bugs, hidden interactions and the possibility that a little systemic complexity avoids a much larger internal burden. - **inferred**: Validity, availability and inclusion triage, unnamed. Ask three separate questions: was the state transition valid, can independent parties obtain enough data to reconstruct or challenge it, and can a censored user get a transaction included? A validity proof answers the first under its cryptographic assumptions, not the second or third. Fraud proofs also need available inputs, a timely honest challenger and a usable dispute path. Data-availability sampling combines coding, random requests and reconstruction; it needs enough participating clients. Apply this checklist to every scaling proposal before assigning it a security label. - **documented**: Rollup, validium and Plasma selection. Start with the asset and its required withdrawal guarantee. Rollups publish sufficient data on the base chain and use validity or challenge mechanisms; validiums prove execution but can leave funds stuck if data disappears. Plasma can give owners a challenged exit from older available state, but shared or ownerless EVM state and mass exits are difficult. Use a weaker system only when its cheaper data and latency justify the residual risks for that application. Do not generalize a successful payment exit game to every liquidity pool, collateral position or smart contract. - **inferred**: Emergency-exit capacity, unnamed. Calculate simultaneous users or objects needing exit, gas per exit, available base-layer capacity and the actual dispute deadline. The simple feasibility test is N ≤ C × D, where N is required exits, C is exit capacity per unit time and D is the deadline. Then separately test affordability: nominal capacity is not useful to users priced out during a crisis. In his 2025 illustration, a week supports about 7.56 million 120,000-gas exits or 121 million idealized 7,500-gas exits. These are scenario estimates, not guaranteed capacity. - **documented**: Economic finality and weak subjectivity. First specify the slashable act and what evidence proves it. In the 2017 BFT description, conflicting finalizations imply at least one-third of stake violated a slashable rule, while two-thirds can plausibly finalize under the stated rules. Then examine long-range attacks: once deposits are withdrawn, old keys may sign alternative histories without current stake at risk. New or long-offline nodes therefore need a recent authenticated checkpoint. Use this distinction when comparing PoW, PoS and light clients; attack loss, required attack capital and social recovery are different quantities. - **inferred**: Consensus assumptions audit, unnamed. State whether the model assumes synchrony, bounded clock skew, online observers, a particular honest fraction, or participants who refuse bribes. Test safety and liveness separately under partitions and delayed messages. - **inferred**: Finality-participation-latency trade-off, unnamed. Choose desired validator participation, time to economic finality and affordable node overhead; then calculate signature and networking costs. Small committees reduce work but can lower the cost of repeated attacks because only participating attackers are directly accountable. Candidate responses include stronger aggregation, slowly rotating overlapping committees and two-tier staking, each with different complexity and concentration risks. In 2024 Buterin explored approximately one-slot finality and a 1 ETH minimum as goals, not deployed parameters. Compare attack loss and solo-staker accessibility before adopting a faster design. - **documented**: Client-diversity risk thresholds. Measure implementation share by stake rather than merely counting clients. A failure below one-third can be contained without stopping finality under the article's assumptions; a faulty client between one-third and two-thirds can stop finality while blocks continue. Independent implementations reduce correlated implementation risk and monopoly power, but divergent implementations can themselves split consensus. Use this to set migration priorities, test releases and evaluate common dependencies. Multiple names are not genuine diversity if teams share the same vulnerable code, proof assumptions or operational infrastructure. - **documented**: Multidimensional resource pricing. Identify separately constrained resources, their safe per-block limits and desirable average targets. A single weighted gas sum treats them as interchangeable and can exclude safe combinations or permit dangerous concentration. Separate base fees and caps can improve utilization, but introduce multidimensional block packing, wallet complexity and difficult EVM subcall accounting. The 2024 example uses 1,000 average state accesses and a 2,000-access block limit as illustrations. Apply separate dimensions where worst-case bottlenecks are genuinely independent; do not assume every added dimension is worth its compatibility cost. - **documented**: ZK-EVM compatibility spectrum. Specify what must remain equivalent before comparing prover speed. Type 1 preserves Ethereum execution consensus, supporting reuse for L1 verification but making existing unfriendly operations expensive to prove. Type 2 preserves EVM behavior while changing external structures; type 2.5 reprices expensive operations; type 3 changes or omits some behavior; type 4 compiles high-level languages to a different target. The types are trade-off points, not a quality ranking; the long-term preference described in 2022 was multiple Type 1 implementations. - **inferred**: Proof engineering pipeline, unnamed. Define the exact statement and public inputs; encode computation as algebraic constraints; separately bind initial and final conditions; commit before deriving unpredictable challenges; and verify the degree or proximity assumptions needed for soundness. Naive random checks of execution steps miss isolated errors. Use this framework to interrogate a proof-system design rather than merely asking whether it uses a SNARK or STARK. - **documented**: Quadratic funding and anti-collusion. Calculate a project's allocation as the square of the sum of the square roots of individual contributions; a matching pool supplies the difference. Ten independent one-dollar contributions produce a 100-dollar allocation in the illustrative unconstrained formula. Use it to aggregate dispersed support for public goods, with proportional scaling if the matching budget is limited. - **documented**: Concave versus convex governance. Ask whether a compromise between two choices is better than a random choice between them.
