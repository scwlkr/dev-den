# Vitalik Buterin: frameworks

Built from 57 accepted distinct works and 250,874 source words. Compiled reasoning with source support and limits below.

- **documented**: Trust-model inventory — named. For each security property, identify the actors, how many must behave correctly, their incentives, and the damage if the assumption fails. Distinguish a replaceable honest actor in a 1-of-N system from reliance on one particular provider; separate safety from liveness. Apply this to an L2 by listing who can steal, freeze, censor, or help exit. Do not automatically optimize one component to zero external trust if code review, software distribution, or another component remains the dominant dependency. No universal numerical score is specified; prioritize the weakest assumptions and highest-consequence failures. - **documented**: Scalability trilemma — named. Evaluate throughput beyond an ordinary node, accessible participation, and resistance to a substantial attacker separately. A simple full-node design constrains throughput; a high-throughput design can price out validators; independent chains can divide security. In the 2021 illustration, resistance above 25% was considered acceptable and 5% clearly insufficient, not a universal protocol threshold. Use sharding, availability sampling, and proofs to change the architecture rather than merely raise parameters. Then check actual bandwidth, storage, minimum participant count, and censorship resistance: indirect verification is not free. - **documented**: Validity and data availability decomposition — unnamed. First ask whether the state transition is correct; then ask whether enough data was published for others to reconstruct state and exercise their rights. A validity proof addresses the first, not automatically the second. Fraud proofs need available inputs and timely challengers; a publisher can frustrate a challenge about withheld data by revealing it selectively. Data availability sampling uses erasure coding and random requests, with sufficiently many online samplers and verification that coding is correct. Apply this separation before calling an L2 secure or treating content-addressed storage as availability consensus. - **documented**: L2 exit and connectedness test — unnamed. Start with an L1-issued asset and ask whether a user can withdraw against hostile operators, whether data withholding can freeze the asset, and which application states are actually covered. Then inspect the reverse direction: can the other chain correctly read Ethereum, including reorganizations and exceptional upgrades? A signature-only bridge can accept an invalid transition; a validity proof can prevent that failure without solving withholding. Rollups, validiums, Plasma, and sidechains therefore occupy different points, not interchangeable security categories. - **documented**: Plasma exit-game analysis — named. Identify independently owned objects, the data each owner must retain, who can challenge an invalid exit, and the deadline and L1 capacity needed for collective exit. The 2023 Plasma Cash example uses a seven-day response and challenge window; these are design-specific, not universal. Fungible assets can fragment into costly exits, while shared EVM state may have no single responsible owner. A proof linking balances to a parallel UTXO graph can broaden protection, but CDPs and pools still need special logic. Prefer rollups when their stronger general security and developer experience justify data costs. - **documented**: Accountable finality and weak subjectivity — named concepts. Separate fork choice from finality, then ask what signed evidence identifies an attack and what stake can actually be penalized. In the 2017 BFT model, conflicting finalizations imply at least one-third violated a slashable rule; two-thirds can plausibly finalize under the stated conditions. Deposits eventually unlock, so long-range histories require recent trusted checkpoints for new or long-offline nodes. Censorship is different: non-inclusion is hard to prove, and recovery may require social coordination. Do not present slashing as an automatic remedy for every attack. - **documented**: Consensus assumption audit — unnamed. Specify timing, clock bounds, validator selection, observer behavior, and the required honest share before interpreting a fault-tolerance percentage. In the 2018 overlay illustration, a 512-validator random committee with 5% honest stake has roughly a one-in-a-trillion chance of containing no honest participant, conditional on the sampling assumptions and timing bound. Observers must actively watch. If timing and underlying threshold assumptions fail together or in consecutive rounds, the overlay can break. Use the construction for an explicit deployment model, not as evidence that asynchronous consensus or offline users escape impossibility bounds. - **documented**: Validator-count, finality, and overhead trade-off — unnamed. Set desired participation, finality latency, and the amount of signature processing a node can afford. Compare full-set aggregation, rotating Orbit committees, and two-tier staking on cryptographic complexity, attack cost, and concentration within each tier. A small committee can weaken economic finality because only participating attackers may be identifiable and slashable. - **documented**: Implementation-diversity fault budget — unnamed. Measure client shares and ask whether a bug causes invalid agreement, chain disagreement, or non-finality. In the 2023 analysis, failure of a client below one-third share need not halt the network; a faulty client between one-third and two-thirds can stop finality while blocks continue. Diversity also reduces monopoly influence but introduces consensus-split risks. His 2021 account says operators switched clients during the 2016 Shanghai denial-of-service attacks; this is evidence of useful diversity, not proof that every independent implementation will fail independently. - **documented**: Encapsulated versus systemic complexity — named. Map complexity inside a subsystem separately from assumptions and interactions exposed across the whole protocol. BLS pairings are mathematically complicated but offer a comparatively simple aggregation interface; fraud proofs may use simpler internal machinery while imposing challenge timing, incentives, censorship defenses, and withdrawal liquidity on the system. Use this framework when comparing designs, not as a rule that advanced cryptography is always safer. Larger implementations can contain bugs, interfaces can leak complexity, and a little systemic complexity may sometimes avoid much greater internal complexity. - **documented**: Multidimensional resource pricing — named. Identify independently scarce resources, set each an average target and, where necessary, a worst-case limit, then adjust separate base fees. This admits combinations a scalar gas limit excludes while containing individual bottlenecks. The 2024 state-access illustration uses 1,000 accesses as an average and 2,000 as a block limit; those are examples. Compare a full multidimensional market with simpler max-style charging, which can incentivize bundling. Include builder packing, wallet fee estimation, EVM subcall guarantees, and censorship by filling one resource dimension in the decision. - **documented**: Fee-market control loop — named through EIP-1559. In the coauthored specification, compare parent-block gas use with a target, adjust the base fee, allow temporary expansion, burn the base fee, and pay a capped priority fee to the block producer. The specified elasticity is 2 and the adjustment denominator is 8. Wallets must bound total spending, and clients must handle maximum-size bursts, not merely average blocks. The authors argue burning reduces incentives to manipulate the base fee; their cartel analysis relies on decentralized mining and profitable defection, rather than proving manipulation impossible. - **documented**: Production, validation, and inclusion separation — unnamed. Ask who builds blocks, who verifies them, and who can force transactions into the system. Specialized builders can reduce validator economies of scale but create concentrated selection and censorship power. Validity proofs, availability sampling, decentralized validators, and inclusion channels can constrain that power; none alone guarantees uninterrupted production. In the 2024 FOCIL discussion, all k inclusion-list creators must censor to delay a transaction by one block under the described model, while builders still influence ordering. Cross-domain MEV may concentrate builders even across many rollups. - **documented**: ZK-EVM compatibility spectrum — named. Select the compatibility target before optimizing proving. Type 1 preserves Ethereum execution-layer consensus behavior and supports L1 verification, but inherits expensive components. Type 2 preserves EVM behavior while changing surrounding structures; direct historical-proof users may break. Type 2.5 reprices expensive operations; Type 3 changes or omits some behavior; Type 4 compiles high-level languages to a proof-friendly target but can change bytecode, addresses, and tooling. These are trade-off categories, not quality grades. His 2022 long-term preference was multiple Type 1 implementations, conditional on proving and protocol improvements. - **documented**: Stateless verification and witness budgeting — unnamed. Require a block to supply the accessed state and a proof, then bound adversarial worst-case witness size and proving time. Binary trees, code chunking, and resource repricing can shrink witnesses; the 2024 illustration reduces a worst case from roughly 330 MB to 10.4 MB before further compression. - **documented**: Proof-system design from workload — unnamed. Express execution as constraints with boundary conditions, commit before drawing unpredictable random challenges, and check algebraic identities rather than replaying every step. Select the field and proof construction for the actual data: small fields reduce work on bits and counters, while extension fields provide sufficiently large challenge spaces. Binius packs binary data; circle STARKs use a different structure to make a small field usable for folding. Simplified tutorials omit substantial implementation and soundness details, so benchmark memory movement, proof size, and worst-case behavior before choosing a system. - **documented**: Enshrinement decision — named. Compare application-layer experimentation with protocol-level efficiency, shared fixed costs, censorship resistance, recovery responsibility, and governance burden. Native account-abstraction operations can become visible to inclusion mechanisms, but native rules can also become overly prescriptive. An enshrined ZK-EVM needs data availability, independent implementations, and sufficiently fast proving; encrypted mempools were premature in the 2023 assessment because available trust assumptions were not satisfactory. - **documented**: Emergency-capacity calculation — unnamed. Convert each safety mechanism into actual L1 transactions, gas per operation, available capacity, deadline, and expected simultaneous demand; separately calculate affordability. In the 2025 illustration, a 120,000-gas censorship bypass costs $4.50 at 15 gwei and $2,500 per ETH. Under an uncertain unit-elasticity assumption, reaching below $1 suggests about 4.5 times scaling. - **documented**: Quadratic funding — named. For each public-good project, aggregate contributions using the square of the sum of their square roots, with matching funds supplying the gap beyond donations. The mechanism, coauthored by Buterin, Zoe Hitzig, and E. Glen Weyl in 2018, gives broad participation a different influence from a single large contribution. The first-best result is conditional on the paper's standard model; the supplied abstract does not establish its full assumptions. Before deployment, budget for matching, anti-Sybil checks, collusion defenses, and governance of the community whose preferences the mechanism aggregates.
