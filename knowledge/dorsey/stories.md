# Jack Dorsey: stories

- **documented**: Dispatch maps and incremental learning. Dorsey wanted to visualize movement in a city, so he learned enough programming to draw a map, move dots, constrain them to streets and attach meaning from dispatch reports. His later insight connected transactional, low-latency dispatch to individuals reporting their own status. The transferable lesson is to use a concrete objective to sequence learning; he later qualified his early minimum-knowledge approach by recommending deeper study of tools. - **documented**: A prototype shelved until distribution changed. Dorsey’s early mobile broadcast experiment found little interest, and friends lacked compatible BlackBerry devices. He returned to the concept when interoperable SMS reached many more phones; he and another engineer then built a Twitter prototype in two weeks. The case separates the merit of an idea from timing, distribution and audience readiness. SMS reach helped, but did not establish that the earlier content itself had been compelling. - **documented**: Users turn status updates into conversation. Dorsey says Twitter users began addressing one another with an at-sign, grouping topics with hashtags and manually forwarding posts. He wrote code to recognize replies, while the team made the conventions more accessible. The important move was to infer the underlying desire for conversation and discovery rather than defend the original broadcast design. The same new conversational surface also created problems that the original product did not anticipate. - **documented**: The open Twitter API and outages. Dorsey recalls that a permissive early API let third parties build surprising clients and helped growth, including in Japan. It also repeatedly overloaded Twitter because the team had not conceived of rate limits or comparable protections. His role was that of founder and retrospective critic, not the sole cause of every failure. The case supports combining developer freedom with capacity controls; it does not justify later closure of third-party businesses, which he separately calls a miss. - **documented**: Twitter outages become Square instrumentation. Dorsey says early Twitter lacked visibility into service behavior, producing speculative diagnoses and arguments during crashes. He carried the lesson into Square by making an administrative dashboard an early piece of his own code. Shared metrics helped engineering and operations discuss observable conditions. A dashboard is not itself a reliability program, but this case explains why he treats instrumentation as foundational rather than a later reporting feature. - **documented**: A critical employee holds the system in their head. During repeated Twitter outages, Dorsey says he delayed a difficult firing for roughly six months because one employee possessed operational knowledge but did not work well with others. After the departure, other leaders emerged and the team developed better practices for staying up. His lesson concerns a dangerous knowledge and collaboration bottleneck, not a general instruction to remove difficult specialists without first understanding the dependency. - **documented**: Square’s lost sale and audio-jack reader. Jim McKelvey’s lost glass-art sale gave Dorsey and the team a concrete goal: let a seller accept a card on a phone. Dorsey says card photography felt awkward and the phone’s 30-pin connector carried a licensing cost, so the team used the microphone jack and software decoding. The end-to-end prototype combined hardware, client and server work. Its design is historically specific; today’s payment interfaces, security and device constraints cannot be inferred from it. - **documented**: Merchant access reframed around transactions. Dorsey says Square encountered credit checks that excluded new sellers even though their immediate need was payment acceptance. The team shifted toward admitting merchants and watching transactions for suspicious activity, with people reviewing cases and possible removal. He reports a large rise in acceptance. The lesson is to question whether an inherited risk proxy matches the task; the stated acceptance figures and the full risk outcomes are not independently established here. - **documented**: Proving a payments model to partners. Dorsey describes Square expanding through increasingly large cohorts—approximately 500, 1,000, 10,000 and 50,000 people—and showing results to bank and card-network partners before constraints were relaxed. His role was to help build confidence in a model that depended on incumbent infrastructure. The case illustrates staged evidence under partner risk constraints, not a universal rollout schedule or evidence that those exact cohort sizes fit another system. - **documented**: Square Wallet versus Cash App. Dorsey describes Wallet’s hands-free experience as useful to devoted customers but limited by merchant coverage: payment adoption required both sides to move together. He later regretted shutting it down after roughly three years, contrasting that decision with persistence through several difficult years for Cash App. The cases show both the value of patience and the constraint of two-sided distribution; neither proves Wallet would have succeeded with more time. - **documented**: Bitcoin experiments expose a different user need. Square’s early online seller acceptance experiment saw little activity. Dorsey says a later hack-week prototype connected more directly to Bitcoin and explored paying for coffee with conversion to dollars; he credited Mike Brock with most of that work. In subsequent Cash App design, explicit sale before spending provided a psychological pause instead of automatic conversion. The sequence produced learning, not proof that the first merchant use case had demand. - **documented**: Independent open-source work returns value. Dorsey says Spiral engineers were funded to choose work that strengthened Bitcoin, without an expectation that Block would directly use it. They chose Lightning and built the Lightning Development Kit; Cash App later selected it for its integration. He uses the case to argue that ecosystem support can produce unexpected internal benefits without directing the project from the outset. One favorable outcome is not a guaranteed financial return on every open-source grant. - **documented**: Search filtering goes wrong. In 2018 congressional testimony, Dorsey acknowledged that a criterion using followers’ behavior unfairly filtered about 600,000 accounts from autocomplete and latest results. He said Twitter corrected it and stopped using a related search-filtering signal. The case makes model inputs and downstream outcomes matters for review, not just stated intent. The number and remediation are his contemporaneous testimony, not an independent audit. - **documented**: A ban can be right for a company and wrong for the internet. Dorsey retrospectively judged Twitter’s suspension of Donald Trump correct for the public-company business at the time but wrong for the internet and society. He connected the tension to concentrated authority and advertiser pressure, and criticized his own investment in company-controlled rather than user-controlled conversation tools. This is his assessment of the decision’s competing consequences, not a complete resolution of the safety or legal questions. - **documented**: Nostr portability becomes tangible. Dorsey says that using the same secret key in another client let his identity and data move with him, making interoperability concrete rather than abstract. He treats a network of small, competing applications as an alternative to one company’s all-encompassing app. He also acknowledges that the experience is less convenient for many people and that discovery remains difficult; key portability does not remove the responsibility and risk of managing a secret key. - **documented**: A correction of organizational delegation. Dorsey says separate CEOs for Square and Cash App produced divergent cultures and execution and obscured the value of connecting the two sides of the financial network. He frames this as a personal mistake of delegating too much, despite also advocating agency and distributed decisions. The lesson is to inspect whether delegated units retain a coherent shared purpose and feedback loop; the account does not establish that separate leadership is always ineffective.
