# Jack Dorsey: positions

Built from 48 accepted distinct works and 436,590 source words. Compiled reasoning with source support and limits below.

- **documented**: Developer platforms. Dorsey values APIs because outsiders can build clients, integrations and niche workflows the platform owner would not anticipate or prioritize. His early Twitter experience also showed that ordinary, nonmalicious developer traffic can overwhelm a service. Openness therefore needs capacity protection and operational controls; it is not a reason to leave shared infrastructure unbounded. He later criticized shutting down third-party Twitter businesses as a missed opportunity. - **documented**: Internal and external API parity. In a 2022 interview rebroadcast in 2024, Dorsey endorsed using the same API for Square’s own customer-facing products and outside developers. He credited Alyssa with the idea and said it initially slowed development but later compounded speed and exposed internal teams to developer pain. The lesson applies where an API is a genuine product surface; the evidence does not establish that every internal capability should be public. - **documented**: Product discovery. Observe unexpected behavior, infer the job behind it and reduce friction around the recurring practice. Dorsey credits users with inventing Twitter’s mentions, hashtags and manual retweets; the company made them easier to use. This is not a rule to implement every request: he distinguishes observing patterns and forming a product judgment from treating customer feedback as a complete roadmap. - **documented**: Prototypes and production engineering. Dorsey describes himself as a hacker who wants to make an idea tangible and learn from using it. He distinguishes that work from engineering for stability, scale and safe failure, and says stronger engineers quickly replaced his original Square code. A working demo is a way to expose assumptions, not proof of production readiness. - **documented**: Observability and reliability. Twitter’s early outages and missing instrumentation taught him that teams need shared visibility into resource use and user activity before they can diagnose failures instead of arguing from speculation. He carried the lesson to Square by writing an administrative dashboard early. For payments, the cost of downtime or confusing behavior falls directly on sellers, making operational visibility part of the customer experience. - **documented**: Simplicity and scope. Dorsey favors a coherent utility with a small number of well-executed details over a product that tries to do everything. He treats design, engineering and reliability as parts of the same experience: technology should recede while a person completes the task. This is a stated product goal, not evidence that complexity disappears; in his Square account, the company often absorbs complexity on the seller’s behalf. - **documented**: Constraints should serve a purpose. Twitter’s original 140-character limit reserved 20 characters within a 160-character SMS for a username, reducing split messages and encouraging immediate expression. Later, after the move to 280, Dorsey said many broadcasts remained short while replies used additional space for context. His position is not that the original number was sacred: judge a constraint against both infrastructure and observed behavior. - **documented**: Payments architecture. Square’s early aim was to enable a sale, not merely read a card. Dorsey described Square as an aggregator using an acquiring bank, rather than becoming a bank, and stressed the work spanning hardware, networks, fraud, software and support. His later Bitcoin thesis contrasts wrapping legacy rails in a simpler interface with potentially replacing the underlying foundation; the latter is a proposal, not proof that the former is unnecessary. - **documented**: Custody and keys. Dorsey wants people to have non-custodial options, even when a company also operates a custodial exchange.
