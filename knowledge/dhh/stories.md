# David Heinemeier Hansson (DHH): stories

Built from 200 accepted distinct works and 505,611 source words. Compiled reasoning with source support and limits below.

- **documented**: Ruby and the first Basecamp: As the programmer on the early product, DHH had a known PHP route but gave Ruby a two-week feasibility experiment: access a database, put output on screen and render HTML. He committed in less than a week. Basecamp's client-work problem was concrete, and designers and product collaborators also contributed; his reported 400 programming hours were not the entire company's effort. The case illustrates a bounded spike against real requirements, not a controlled proof that Ruby beats PHP for every team. - **documented**: Rails as extraction: While building Basecamp, DHH created missing Ruby tools for database access, HTML and email, then recognized a reusable toolkit and prepared it for release. In 2004 he described Action Pack and Active Record as the initial components; later accounts emphasize documentation and examples as part of making the release usable. The lesson is to generalize from exercised application needs, not to assume every useful framework was designed fully in advance. Rails subsequently grew far beyond its initial scope. ([dhh-1c832e72fb5bec](https://learning.acm.org/binaries/content/assets/leaning-center/bytecast-transcripts/acm_byte_cast_david_heinemeier_hansson_episode_23_mix---transcript.pdf) / evidence dhh-1c832e72fb5bec-581eb153571d; [dhh-f85670e0025fd9](https://signalvnoise.com/archives/000766) / evidence dhh-f85670e0025fd9-eb49faec78f5; [dhh-9411f0d003fac7](https://jlaine.net/2005/11/02/interview-with-david-heinemeier-hansson/) / evidence dhh-9411f0d003fac7-ff0e357de12c; 3 independent supporting works)

- **documented**: The Merb split: As Rails leader, DHH came to see that contributors needed to feel heard and that public exchanges influenced observers as well as the immediate participants. He acknowledges his own crude manner and poor communication about openness to concerns such as extensibility and performance. The projects eventually merged after finding compatibility despite different priorities. The lesson is not to accept every contribution, but to distinguish protecting a framework's direction from making disagreement socially costly. - **documented**: A shared service became a coordination burden: DHH describes Basecamp's standalone text-document service being shared by three applications that evolved at different rates. Operating it and keeping dependencies synchronized added work beyond the apparent benefit of reuse. He draws a distinction between a method call and a network call, with its own deployment and failure concerns. This experience supports skepticism about unnecessary service boundaries; it does not establish that a shared service is never appropriate. - **documented**: Basecamp's product-driven rebuild: DHH says the team first tried to adapt its existing application to a new design; for Basecamp 3 the exploration lasted roughly two weeks before they judged the old chassis unsuitable. They began with a small group and kept the existing product alive rather than forcing all in-flight work to migrate. The practical lesson is to test retrofit against the intended product and preserve customer continuity. A clean rewrite sacrifices accumulated fixes and imposes parallel maintenance costs. - **documented**: An attachment-loss bug changed the testing argument: DHH recounts a Basecamp editing flow in which a customer could lose an attachment even though the individual units were well tested. The missing protection was a system test for that particular assembled flow. His lesson is to buy confidence at the layer where users experience the failure, not to count unit tests as a guarantee of correct integration. A system suite still cannot establish certainty or cover every possible failure. - **documented**: From Webpacker to import maps: DHH built Webpacker because the browser ecosystem once made a JavaScript compilation pipeline a worthwhile bridge. Later, as browser capabilities improved, he tested import maps in HEY before making them a Rails default; the migration exposed timing issues in Turbo Native integration. The case shows how to validate a simplifying default in a consequential application and retain a fallback for applications needing compilation. HEY is one production case, not proof that every frontend can avoid a build. - **documented**: HEY Feed pagination: The team faced the cost of rendering multiple isolated HTML emails at once, particularly on weaker devices. DHH describes changing the initial load to two messages, loading six asynchronously, and increasing later page sizes as the reader scrolls. A Stimulus controller observes the pagination link and appends the same server response shape. The lesson is to optimize the work users wait for, not necessarily eliminate all work. The cited article gives the design, not an independently measured benchmark. - **documented**: Cloud exit and Kamal: DHH had embraced cloud rental, then joined the 37signals team in pursuing owned hardware after the cost and staffing assumptions disappointed them. A Kubernetes-provider detour led him to build Kamal; the team used VMs and containers, moved lower-criticality applications first, and staged HEY's components separately. He reports the operations team did not grow, with external staff handling physical work. Existing expertise, spare rack capacity and predictable demand are essential limits on transferring the economics. - **documented**: Passkeys removed, then reconsidered: The team implemented passkey-only login for ONCE/Campfire and removed it after finding cross-device access and the resulting experience unsatisfactory relative to the backend complexity. The operational lesson is to assess the complete recovery and device journey, not declare a security mechanism good or bad in isolation. - **documented**: HEY contact-page scope negotiation: DHH recounts an engineering discussion in which a thread-level implementation could have taken half a day, but Jason Fried judged it insufficient for the feature's essential purpose. The team took a longer path lasting two weeks. DHH's role was to surface the technical shortcut and its cost; the product decision was not his alone. The case limits simplistic advice to always choose the fastest implementation: cheap scope is valuable only if it delivers the intended outcome. - **documented**: HEY and the App Store: DHH opposed mandatory platform billing for both its commission and its effect on refunds, discounts and the direct customer relationship. The initial compromise used temporary accounts, creating an awkward onboarding experience. Later external-payment linking let HEY move toward real signup and web subscription flows in the described US context. He nevertheless acknowledges platform billing can be attractive to small developers who want help with tax and customer service. The case is his account of platform constraints, not legal advice. - **documented**: Sales-tax cleanup: DHH acknowledges a limit to keeping the company lean. After uncollected US sales tax produced a lengthy and costly cleanup, he concluded 37signals should have added finance expertise earlier and reports hiring a head of finance. His account puts the cleanup and settlements at roughly two years and $5 million. The lesson for technical teams is to examine consequential specialist risks rather than treating low headcount as a goal that overrides every neglected obligation.
