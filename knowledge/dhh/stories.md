# David Heinemeier Hansson (DHH): stories

Built from 79 accepted distinct works and 321,087 source words. Compiled reasoning with source support and limits below.

- **documented**: Basecamp and Rails under a time constraint. DHH was the programmer on the early product, working roughly ten hours a week while designers also contributed. Email and FTP had made client-project context difficult to share, so the team sought a product people could use without extensive training. Rails emerged by extracting reusable infrastructure from that real work. He later reported 400 billed programming hours for the first version. The lesson is to constrain effort around a concrete problem and generalize proven pieces; the hours are not the full design or company effort, nor a transferable estimate. - **documented**: FTP collisions justified a new tool. In early collaboration with designers, DHH repeatedly overwrote their changes and introduced shared version control after the problem recurred. His point is not that solo development and team development have identical needs: a new coordination mechanism can be worthwhile when it removes a demonstrated failure. The limit is equally important: adopting it does not require importing every process of a much larger organization. - **documented**: Basecamp 3 retrofit before rewrite. DHH initially explored adapting the existing application to a substantially different design, including roughly two weeks on a duplicated or branched codebase. The team concluded that the existing structure did not support the desired product well enough and began clean, while keeping useful shared capabilities. This illustrates a product-driven rewrite after a concrete feasibility test, not permission to throw away a codebase simply because its style is disliked. - **documented**: An attachment-loss bug despite passing unit tests. DHH recounts a Basecamp editing flow in which a customer could lose an attachment even though individual units were well tested. The missing coverage was a system test for that particular interaction. His lesson is to choose test boundaries for the failure you need to catch, not equate isolated test success with assembled-product correctness. One incident does not establish that unit tests are useless or that system tests alone are sufficient. - **documented**: A shared service increased coordination. DHH describes a reusable text-document service used by three applications that changed at different rates. Operating the service and keeping dependencies synchronized became a recurring cost. It does not establish that every shared service is a mistake: a boundary still needs to be judged against its independent operational and organizational benefits. - **documented**: Cloud exit by criticality ladder. DHH and the operations team used a low-criticality application to settle questions such as database placement before moving more consequential services. For HEY, they moved components in stages rather than making a single cutover; proximity between cloud and colocation helped make partial moves practical. The lesson is to buy information and preserve rollback options before increasing blast radius. Their retained operations expertise, existing facilities and favorable network geography limit direct transfer. - **documented**: HEY contact-page scope negotiation. DHH recounts asking Jason Fried whether a simple thread-level implementation would satisfy a feature showing a person’s email history. The shortcut could have taken half a day, but Fried judged it insufficiently useful; the chosen path took two weeks. - **documented**: Sales-tax cleanup exposed the cost of staying too lean. DHH says uncollected US sales tax led to roughly two years and $5 million in settlements and cleanup, after which the company added finance leadership. The lesson for a small technical organization is not to maximize headcount, but to identify expertise whose absence creates a large accumulated liability. The reported cost is his account of a specific business failure, not a general staffing threshold. - **documented**: HEY billing and onboarding. DHH opposed mandatory platform billing not only over the fee but because refunds, discounts and a cross-platform customer relationship would become more difficult. The initial compromise used awkward temporary accounts; a later US external-payment flow allowed real signup and web subscription. He also recognizes that in-app purchasing can be useful to small developers who do not want to manage international taxes or customer service. The platform and legal circumstances described are specific, not a worldwide rule. - **documented**: Rails progress versus upgrade pain. In the doctrine, DHH defended accepting some breaking change for long-term improvement, while acknowledging that the Rails 2-to-3 transition left users behind. Later experience with frontend churn led him to say he would phrase that principle differently and place more value on continuity. This case shows a maintained project leader revising a value in response to users’ cumulative upgrade burden; it is not a claim that Rails should never break compatibility.
