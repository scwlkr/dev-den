List the assumptions behind the current plan, identify which input has changed, and reconsider the downstream branches rather than merely patching the latest decision. - **inferred**: Unnamed: Decide with incomplete information, adjusted for reversibility. Lütke describes acting at roughly 70% understanding and separately cites a 40–70% information heuristic. Inferred guidance: state the next observation that could change the decision and set a review point before committing scarce time. - **inferred**: Unnamed: First-principles comparison, including the status quo. - **inferred**: Minimum lovable product: Limit the initial scope, not the care invested in the experience. Identify the small audience and job for which the release should be genuinely useful and appealing, then preserve a quality bar within that boundary. - **inferred**: Unnamed: Research, prototype, discard, then build. Learn the user's domain before settling on implementation; Lütke's 90/10 research-to-building description is illustrative rather than a universal time budget. Constrain prototype time and staffing. - **inferred**: Unnamed: Build the tool before stretching the feature. When an implementation is awkward because the underlying primitive is missing, consider improving the framework or internal tool so this and related problems become easier. Lütke describes improving Rails while building Shopify. Compare the one-off delivery benefit with the investment and future reuse of the better primitive. The qualification matters: he also says Shopify has built things internally that it had no business building. - **documented**: Unnamed: Reproduce the failure, specify the invariant, fix the cause. In his cart example, Lütke first wrote a functional test reproducing the production exception: add an item, delete its product, and render the cart. He then wrote a failing unit test for the desired association behavior before changing the relationship and rerunning tests. The distinction is operational: the rendering error was a symptom; deletion semantics were the cause. This is a worked historical example, not proof that every incident can be reproduced in a unit test. For obscure production paths, he also values logs that preserve preceding context. - **inferred**: Unnamed: Make failure ordinary enough to exercise recovery. Treat packet loss, server failure, and missing communication as properties of a system rather than exceptional impossibilities. Lütke describes deliberately switching off production servers to reveal dependencies and later randomizing failures. Evaluate whether redundancy and recovery actually work, not just whether a component stayed up. Inferred guidance: bound the blast radius and ensure an accountable response before conducting disruption; the supplied account does not establish a particular safety procedure. Antifragility is his conceptual influence, not a guarantee that any disruption improves a system. - **inferred**: Unnamed: Small teams with an editor. Use one person when the entire problem fits their skills and capacity; otherwise favor a small, cross-skilled team with a clear decision-maker who preserves the overall product outcome. Lütke variously describes roughly four or five people, with growth to about eight prompting a split or being temporary. Those are Shopify heuristics, not universal thresholds. - **inferred**: Unnamed: Compass metric with proxy safeguards. In 2013, Lütke advised choosing a metric that reflects business health, designing a feedback interval short enough for experiments, and judging results against that compass rather than convenient early signals alone. Maintain a set of prompts with expected or judged outcomes, especially tasks that earlier models failed; rerun them as models change and use failures as future evaluations. - **inferred**: Unnamed: Decision journal and retrospective. For a major choice, record the decision and the decisive information available at the time. Lütke describes revisiting such notes about every six months. When the outcome is bad, ask whether the problem was luck or whether a modest additional effort could have uncovered an accessible missing input; he uses 20 minutes as a personal retrospective prompt, not a universal research limit. Review the reasoning, not just the outcome, and update the decision when its assumptions change. - **inferred**: Unnamed: Find the disagreement's divergence point. Ask which underlying assumption, piece of context, or expected outcome makes two people favor different choices. Lütke invites challenges to his own view and sometimes argues the other side to test an idea. If persuaded, update the model; if a project is genuinely unlikely to succeed, he prefers stopping or redirecting it over letting people continue without challenge. His qualification is important: abrupt reversals can create whiplash, and provocative disagreement can unnecessarily stress junior or less familiar colleagues.
