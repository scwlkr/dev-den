# Tobias Lütke (Tobi Lütke): frameworks

Built from 21 accepted distinct works and 238,779 source words. Compiled reasoning with source support and limits below.

- **documented**: Product floor, ceiling and distribution. Name: unnamed. Parts: assess how easily a beginner reaches a useful result, whether an expert can achieve an exceptional result, and how many users actually progress beyond the default. Use this when designing platforms, abstractions or AI-assisted workflows. Decision guidance: favor a simpler starting path only if it does not unnecessarily close off advanced outcomes; a theoretical escape hatch is not enough if almost nobody can use it. Inspect actual user outcomes against their own goals. The evidence supplies no numerical acceptance threshold. - **documented**: Core product plus ecosystem. Name: unnamed. Parts: establish a product and active user base; build common needs into the core; expose specialized capabilities to external builders; make participation economically attractive. Use when a growing list of customer-specific requests threatens the main interface. Lütke describes roughly 80 percent as common needs, not a universal measured cutoff. The choice is not simply more features versus fewer: a platform can preserve a comprehensible default while allowing specialized solutions. His economic test is whether outside builders collectively earn more than the platform; he says Shopify reached that point in 2018. - **inferred**: Amortize complexity across users. Name: unnamed. Parts: identify a recurring technical or operational burden, solve it centrally, and distribute the benefit through a shared service. Use when individual customers lack time or expertise for hosting, reliability, tax or logistics. A hosted platform can deploy a fix once and pool investment in performance, but it also assumes responsibility for uptime and scale. Inferred decision guidance: centralize when the shared solution meaningfully reduces repeated effort without making important customer-specific needs impossible; compare the operational burden with the benefit delivered, rather than assuming hosted or open source is always superior. - **inferred**: Customer problem, builder solution. Name: unnamed. Parts: observe customers directly, ask about their difficulties, then use product judgment to design a coherent solution. Lütke describes watching customer interviews, trying store signup and contacting support under pseudonyms. Use when feature requests compete or quantitative reports hide friction. His rule is not to ignore users: customers are valuable witnesses to problems, but simply implementing their requested design can abdicate the builder’s responsibility for the whole product. Inferred check: compare the proposed feature with observed behavior and alternative solutions before committing. - **documented**: Compass metric and feedback loop. Parts: select a metric that reflects underlying business health; choose a timeframe that permits useful feedback; display it clearly; review learning and next actions; reassess the metric as the business changes. In his 2013 example, Shopify used a weekly red/green email and a short meeting. He recommends using other indicators for early readings but judging an experiment by its effect on the compass metric. Daily engaged users can be more revealing than signups for a hypothetical social network. Do not treat the historical weekly cadence or example metric as a universal current rule. - **inferred**: Finite milestones inside an enduring mission. Name: finite and infinite games, adapted from James Carse. Parts: identify the continuing pursuit; define measurable, winnable projects within it; revisit whether those projects still serve the larger purpose. Use when teams need near-term clarity but a metric could become the goal itself. Lütke warns that people can become attached to measurable games, while also recognizing that open-ended missions can be too ambiguous to motivate action. Inferred decision guidance: retain a milestone only while its success remains a credible contribution to the continuing objective. - **documented**: Decision tree rederivation. Name: unnamed. Parts: record the assumptions supporting a plan; identify a foundational input that has changed; return to that point in the decision tree; rederive downstream choices instead of merely patching the old roadmap. Lütke uses COVID restrictions and the assumption of free movement as his example. Use for consequential changes in operating conditions, technology or regulation. Decision guidance: the trigger is an invalidated dependency, not every minor surprise. Record enough context to revisit the decision again; rederivation does not guarantee a correct forecast. - **inferred**: Compare against the real status quo. Name: unnamed. Parts: list benefits and costs of the proposed change and of continuing as things are; estimate uncertain outcomes; make the decision rather than treating a single objection as a veto. Lütke describes applying this to the decision to go public. Use when a new system’s visible risks are compared with an implicitly flawless existing system. Inferred scoring guidance: a qualitative side-by-side comparison is justified; no numerical weights or probability thresholds are supplied. Include second-order effects where plausible, but distinguish speculation from evidence. - **inferred**: Match the method to the abstraction level. Name: unnamed. Parts: determine whether the question is directly measurable; benchmark infrastructure when a performance test can answer it; use uncertain trends and longer-horizon judgment for market choices; connect local work to the broader mission. Use when a team imports strategic speculation into a testable technical decision, or demands a benchmark for an unknowable market future. - **inferred**: Small team with an editor. Name: unnamed. Parts: start with one capable author when the problem fits; otherwise form a small team and designate an editor who understands the intended result, resolves disagreements and preserves product coherence. Lütke has described five as a preferred team size, temporarily flexing to eight; in a later account he described a three-to-five-person bias with AI broadening individual capabilities. - **documented**: Organization as a desired-state system. Name: Shopify OS and reconciliation. - **inferred**: Design incentives as system behavior. Name: unnamed. Parts: identify what participants can gain, how their local choices affect the wider system, and which rules align those interests with the mission. Use for app ecosystems, compensation or organizational processes. - **inferred**: Chesterton’s Fence plus subtraction. Name: established-system review. Parts: discover why an existing mechanism exists; separate embedded knowledge from accidental accumulation; consider removal or rebuilding; preserve useful checks. Use before rewriting a service, deleting a process or reorganizing a department. Lütke warns both that additions compound maintenance costs and that an attempted improvement can make an established system worse. Inferred decision guidance: remove a layer when its purpose and dependencies are understood and the remaining system can satisfy them; do not treat age alone as evidence of waste. - **documented**: Outage as tuition. Name: unnamed. Parts: understand the avoidable mistake, retain and apply the lesson, and judge whether the same failure recurs. Lütke argues against automatically firing someone after an expensive outage: the organization has already paid for the learning, and professional pride may supply substantial self-reproach. His second chance is conditional on not repeating the same failure. Use for recoverable mistakes where learning is possible. Do not generalize this into tolerance for catastrophic risk: he explicitly distinguishes failures involving replaceable resources from risks to human life. - **documented**: Context engineering and a living constitution. Parts: state the task and enough relevant context to make a solution plausible; articulate substantive principles on which reasonable people could disagree; test projects and examples against those principles; revise ambiguous or incorrect principles. Use for agent tasks and human project discussions alike. Lütke credits Anthropic for the constitution terminology and treats the result as a prompt for discussion, not a mechanical control. Decision guidance: a value that nobody would reasonably oppose is too generic to guide a trade-off; a written principle still needs evidence of use in practice.
