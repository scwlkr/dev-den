# Casey Muratori: stories

Built from 36 accepted distinct works and 425,131 source words. Compiled reasoning with source support and limits below.

- **documented**: Granny viewer and immediate-mode UI. Muratori reports experimenting in 2002 while building a cross-platform Granny viewer. Existing GUI approaches scattered a simple interaction across layout machinery, identifiers and callbacks; he tried organizing the interface around an application-controlled update path instead. His retrospective says this simplified the viewer and supported both 2D and 3D interaction. The operational lesson is to inspect where control flow and state actually reside before adding another framework. The 2005 talk describes one implementation, but its displayed code is not preserved in the supplied captions. - **documented**: Granny 1 to Granny 2. Muratori wrote the first character-animation library in an object-oriented, templated style and later judged its integration too restrictive. Animation had to meet rendering, physics and gameplay systems rather than operate as an isolated leaf. He and Jeff Roberts spent over a year redesigning the API; Muratori reports that the second version remained in use for years with relatively minor changes. His later C-first approach admitted features when they demonstrated value. This is a practitioner retrospective, not a controlled comparison proving that all C++ abstractions fail. - **documented**: Granny animation-control bug. Muratori identifies an exception to Granny 2's more open design: a memory-constrained customer needed to recycle control structures. A rushed special feature that was not part of normal testing introduced a difficult bug. In hindsight he thinks more direct control over individual updates could have avoided the special request. The useful lesson is to check whether an opaque API forces exceptional mechanisms and to test such mechanisms explicitly. His account does not establish a single exclusive cause of the bug. - **documented**: The Witness vegetation distribution. Muratori worked on an editor operation that let artists control grass and mesh placement without placing every item by hand. Global random rejection became expensive near saturation and could still leave gaps. He replaced it with a wavefront that samples around accepted points, addressing distribution while reducing the long tail. He estimated an intermediate improvement from roughly 30 seconds to 10–15 seconds but had not timed it because speed was not yet the objective. This was a practical project-scoped method, not a claim of optimal blue-noise generation. - **documented**: The Witness raycast rewrite. Investigating the still-slow planting operation, Muratori stepped through the actual calls and found repeated entity gathering and general mesh-tree traversal for each candidate. Because queries were local and downward, he gathered geometry once, bucketed triangles in a 2D grid and precomputed a cheaper test, then used SIMD for batches. He reports that the result was effectively interactive and mesh updating became the bottleneck. The original reuse was understandable under team constraints; the spoken captions do not supply the code or a complete benchmark. - **documented**: Shape-area benchmark. In his 2023 authored example, Muratori compared a class hierarchy with alternative representations for summing shape areas. A flat array and type switch reduced his reported cost from about 35 to 24 cycles per shape; an operation-oriented coefficient table reached about 3–3.5. The changes exposed shared formulas as well as changing dispatch and data layout, so the result cannot isolate virtual-call cost or predict every hierarchy's performance. His reported AVX variant is not supplied as code, and the numerical ratios belong to this synthetic workload. - **documented**: The missing increment. In a 2024 public investigation, Muratori expected a predictable increment loop to be limited by its serial dependency, yet a remote i7-12700K appeared to exceed that expectation. He separated timestamp-counter ticks from core cycles, checked the possible boost ratio and used ETW counters to corroborate the unusual throughput. He proposed rename-time reassociation as a possible explanation but did not claim knowledge of the chip's implementation. The case teaches measurement cross-checking and hypothesis revision; it does not prove the mechanism or generalize to all Intel processors. - **documented**: Repeated image filtering. In 2019 Muratori tested half-pixel filters by reversing spatial shift every two passes and comparing results until convergence or chosen error limits. His constrained integer filter converged after 208 iterations on the test images, while the listed alternatives eventually crossed a limit. Searching toward a sharper kernel and relaxing the original tap and arithmetic constraints produced a better-looking eight-tap floating-point result in his account. The lesson is to make both the stability test and implementation constraints explicit; the images and limited tests do not prove universal superiority. - **documented**: Metaball editor and property callbacks. As an early game developer, Muratori built a negative-space level modeler using smoothly blended implicit shapes. A selection operation needed to reason across several entities, but his hierarchy kept properties inside them. He constructed parallel collections of type-safe get and set callbacks, with extra machinery for operations such as dragging and undo. In hindsight he considered storing properties in collections addressed by entity ID a simpler alternative. The example is his self-critique and retrospective design, not supplied source code or proof that every editor should use an ECS. - **documented**: Deferred conflicts on a small team. Muratori describes a custom version-control workflow in which each user's edit remains primary locally and conflicting edits are preserved as alternate files. People can save and share first, then reconcile when a central API change or compilation failure makes it necessary. This illustrates separating preservation from immediate conflict resolution. He explicitly does not claim the existing workflow is ready for a much larger organization, where drift and coordination must be studied. No implementation or independent evaluation is supplied. - **documented**: Ultima Underworld 2 entity overlay. Muratori recounts a memory-saving representation in which fields for different entity features overlapped. An empty lantern's fuel could be interpreted as zero hit points, and the same overlap made combining both features impossible without redesign. He uses the case to show that a compact representation encodes assumptions about which properties can coexist. It is his historical account, not a demonstrated reproduction, and the original memory constraints matter when judging the trade-off. - **documented**: Recovering a historical profiling source. While investigating the premature-optimization maxim, Muratori followed a difficult-to-find reference to a physical book and examined the described FORTRAN profiler. He says it estimated costs from execution counts and source syntax rather than generated machine code, and that the reported concentration was closer to 3% of code accounting for 50% of time than the stronger implication he had calculated. The lesson is to trace numerical claims and inspect measurement methods. Both his interpretation and the profiler's own estimates have limitations. - **documented**: ARM1 assumption revised. Muratori says he had heard that early ARM chips were not microcoded, but changed his interpretation after seeing Ken Shirriff's silicon reverse engineering. He now considers ARM1 microcoded while recognizing that the degree and definition can be debated. The case is useful when assessing received hardware explanations: seek evidence about the particular implementation and revise the claim without converting a historical observation into a universal rule about an ISA. - **documented**: Video-codec beta under a disc budget. Muratori reports helping prepare a second-generation codec beta for a Gears of War release that needed better cinematic compression. Jeff Roberts led the codec; Muratori's contributions included SIMD filter generators and a visual debugging utility for inspecting planes and errors.
